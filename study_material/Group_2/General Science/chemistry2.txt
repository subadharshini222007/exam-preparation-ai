# CHEMISTRY – PERIODIC TABLE

### 1. Introduction to the Periodic Table

The Periodic Table is a systematic arrangement of chemical elements in increasing order of their atomic numbers.

Elements are arranged according to their electronic configurations and chemical properties.

The modern periodic table was developed based on the Modern Periodic Law.

**Modern Periodic Law:**

The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

This means that when elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic number, similar chemical properties repeat at regular intervals.

**Important points:**

* Total number of elements: 118.
* Number of periods: 7.
* Number of groups: 18.
* First element: Hydrogen (H).
* Element with atomic number 118: Oganesson (Og).
* Most elements are metals.
* Elements in the same group generally have similar chemical properties because they have similar outer-shell electronic configurations.

**TNPSC Group 2 Exam Point:** The modern periodic table is based on atomic number, not atomic mass.

---

### 2. Development of the Periodic Table

#### 2.1 Dobereiner's Triads

Johann Wolfgang Dobereiner proposed the Law of Triads in 1829.

He arranged certain elements into groups of three having similar chemical properties.

The atomic mass of the middle element was approximately equal to the average atomic masses of the other two elements.

**Example: Lithium, Sodium and Potassium**

| Element   | Symbol | Approximate Atomic Mass |
| --------- | ------ | ----------------------: |
| Lithium   | Li     |                       7 |
| Sodium    | Na     |                      23 |
| Potassium | K      |                      39 |

Formula:

Average atomic mass of the first and third elements:

$$
\frac{7+39}{2}=23
$$

The result is approximately equal to the atomic mass of sodium.

**Limitation:** This law was applicable only to a few groups of elements.

#### 2.2 Newlands' Law of Octaves

John Newlands proposed the Law of Octaves in 1864.

When elements were arranged in increasing order of atomic mass, every eighth element had properties similar to the first element.

**Limitation:** The law was mainly applicable to lighter elements and did not work well for heavier elements.

#### 2.3 Mendeleev's Periodic Table

Dmitri Mendeleev proposed his periodic table in 1869.

He arranged elements primarily in increasing order of atomic mass and grouped elements with similar properties together.

**Achievements:**

1. He left gaps for undiscovered elements.
2. He predicted the properties of some undiscovered elements.
3. He corrected certain atomic masses.
4. His table helped scientists understand relationships among elements.

**Limitation:** Some elements could not be arranged correctly according to increasing atomic mass.

#### 2.4 Modern Periodic Table

Henry Moseley demonstrated the importance of atomic number in classifying elements.

The modern periodic table arranges elements in increasing order of atomic number.

**TNPSC Important Questions:**

* Who proposed the Law of Triads? — Dobereiner.
* Who proposed the Law of Octaves? — Newlands.
* Who developed the periodic table in 1869? — Mendeleev.
* Who established the importance of atomic number in periodic classification? — Henry Moseley.
* What is the basis of the modern periodic table? — Atomic number.

### 3. Structure of the Modern Periodic Table

The periodic table consists of horizontal rows called periods and vertical columns called groups.

#### 3.1 Periods

There are seven periods.

| Period     | Number of Elements |
| ---------- | -----------------: |
| 1st period |                  2 |
| 2nd period |                  8 |
| 3rd period |                  8 |
| 4th period |                 18 |
| 5th period |                 18 |
| 6th period |                 32 |
| 7th period |           Up to 32 |

The first period is the shortest period.

The sixth period includes the lanthanides, while the seventh period includes the actinides.

#### 3.2 Groups

There are 18 groups in the modern periodic table.

| Group | Family                | Examples     |
| ----- | --------------------- | ------------ |
| 1     | Alkali metals         | Li, Na, K    |
| 2     | Alkaline earth metals | Be, Mg, Ca   |
| 3–12  | Transition elements   | Fe, Cu, Zn   |
| 13    | Boron family          | B, Al        |
| 14    | Carbon family         | C, Si        |
| 15    | Nitrogen family       | N, P         |
| 16    | Oxygen family         | O, S         |
| 17    | Halogens              | F, Cl, Br, I |
| 18    | Noble gases           | He, Ne, Ar   |

**Important exception:** Hydrogen is placed in Group 1 because of its electronic configuration, but it has properties different from those of alkali metals.

### 5. Atomic Structure and Important Formulas

To understand the periodic table, first understand atomic structure.

An atom consists of a nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons occupying the surrounding regions.

| Particle | Charge | Relative Mass          |
| -------- | ------ | ---------------------- |
| Proton   | +1     | Approximately 1 u      |
| Neutron  | 0      | Approximately 1 u      |
| Electron | −1     | Approximately 1/1836 u |

#### 5.1 Atomic Number

Atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus.

Formula:

$$
\boxed{Z=\text{Number of Protons}}
$$

For a neutral atom:

$$
\boxed{\text{Number of Protons}=\text{Number of Electrons}}
$$

**Example: Sodium**

Atomic number of sodium = 11.

Therefore:

* Protons = 11.
* Electrons in a neutral sodium atom = 11.

#### 5.2 Mass Number

Mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons.

$$
\boxed{A=Z+N}
$$

Where:

* \(A\) = Mass number.
* \(Z\) = Atomic number.
* \(N\) = Number of neutrons.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{N=A-Z}
$$

**Solved Sum 1**

An atom has atomic number 17 and mass number 35. Find the number of protons, electrons and neutrons.

**Solution:**

Given:

$$
Z=17,\qquad A=35
$$

Protons:

$$
=Z=17
$$

Electrons in a neutral atom:

$$
=17
$$

Neutrons:

$$
N=A-Z=35-17=18
$$

**Answer:** 17 protons, 17 electrons and 18 neutrons.

#### 5.3 Electronic Configuration

Electronic configuration describes how electrons are distributed among the shells of an atom.

The maximum number of electrons in a shell is calculated using:

$$
\boxed{2n^2}
$$

Where \(n\) is the shell number.

| Shell | Shell Number | Maximum Capacity |
| ----- | -----------: | ---------------: |
| K     |            1 |                2 |
| L     |            2 |                8 |
| M     |            3 |               18 |
| N     |            4 |               32 |

These values represent the maximum theoretical capacities of the shells. Actual filling order follows orbital-energy rules, so electrons do not always fill an entire shell before entering the next one.

**Solved Sum 2**

Find the maximum number of electrons in the third shell.

Formula:

$$
2n^2
$$

Substitute \(n=3\):

$$
2(3)^2=2\times9=18
$$

**Answer: 18 electrons.**

| --------- | ------------: | ------------------- |

| Helium    |             2 | 2                   |

**TNPSC concept:**

* The number of occupied shells indicates the period.

It has three occupied shells, so it is in Period 3. It has one outermost-shell electron, consistent with Group 1.

### 6. Valency and Chemical Formulas

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom.

Atoms may gain, lose or share electrons to form chemical bonds.

| Element   | Symbol | Common Valency |
| --------- | ------ | -------------: |
| Hydrogen  | H      |              1 |
| Oxygen    | O      |              2 |
| Nitrogen  | N      |              3 |
| Carbon    | C      |              4 |
| Sodium    | Na     |              1 |
| Magnesium | Mg     |              2 |
| Aluminium | Al     |              3 |
| Chlorine  | Cl     |              1 |
| Calcium   | Ca     |              2 |
| Zinc      | Zn     |              2 |

Some elements show more than one valency or oxidation state. The table lists common introductory examples.

#### 6.1 Writing Chemical Formulas Using Valency

For a simple compound, the valencies can be crossed over to determine the subscripts, then reduced to the smallest whole-number ratio where necessary.

**Example 1: Magnesium Chloride**

Magnesium valency = 2.

Chlorine valency = 1.

Cross the valencies:

$$
Mg^{2+},\ Cl^-
$$

The formula is:

$$
\boxed{MgCl_2}
$$

One magnesium ion combines with two chloride ions to produce a neutral compound.

**Example 2: Aluminium Oxide**

Aluminium valency = 3.

Oxygen valency = 2.

Cross the valencies:

$$
\boxed{Al_2O_3}
$$

**Example 3: Calcium Chloride**

Calcium valency = 2.

Chlorine valency = 1.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{CaCl_2}
$$

**Example 4: Sodium Oxide**

Sodium valency = 1.

Oxygen valency = 2.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{Na_2O}
$$

**Example 5: Calcium Hydroxide**

Calcium ion = \(Ca^{2+}\).

Hydroxide ion = \(OH^-\).

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{Ca(OH)_2}
$$

Brackets are used because two hydroxide ions are needed.

### 8. Periodic Trends

Periodic trends are changes in the properties of elements across a period and down a group.

#### 8.1 Atomic Radius

Atomic radius indicates the approximate size of an atom.

**Across a period:** Atomic radius generally decreases from left to right.

**Down a group:** Atomic radius generally increases from top to bottom.

**Reason:** Across a period, nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same principal shell. Down a group, additional electron shells increase atomic size.

#### 8.2 Ionisation Energy

Ionisation energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom.

General trend:

* Across a period: increases.
* Down a group: decreases.

There are exceptions due to electronic configurations.

#### 8.3 Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract shared electrons.

General trend:

* Across a period: increases.
* Down a group: decreases.

Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

#### 8.4 Metallic Character

Metallic character describes the tendency of an element to lose electrons and form positive ions.

General trend:

* Across a period: decreases.
* Down a group: increases.

#### 8.5 Non-Metallic Character

Non-metallic character generally:

* Increases across a period.
* Decreases down a group.

**TNPSC revision table**

| Property                  | Across a Period     | Down a Group        |
| ------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------- |
| Atomic radius             | Generally decreases | Generally increases |
| Ionisation energy         | Generally increases | Generally decreases |
| Electronegativity         | Generally increases | Generally decreases |
| Metallic character        | Generally decreases | Generally increases |
| Number of occupied shells | Remains the same    | Increases           |

### 9. Important Families in the Periodic Table

#### 9.1 Group 1 – Alkali Metals

Examples: Lithium, Sodium, Potassium.

Properties:

* Have one outermost-shell electron.
* Commonly form +1 ions.
* Are generally soft and reactive metals.
* Reactivity generally increases down the group.
* React with water to form hydroxides and hydrogen gas.

Example reaction:

$$
2Na+2H_2O\rightarrow2NaOH+H_2
$$

Sodium reacts with water to form sodium hydroxide and hydrogen.

#### 9.2 Group 2 – Alkaline Earth Metals

Examples: Beryllium, Magnesium, Calcium.

Properties:

* Have two outermost-shell electrons.
* Commonly form +2 ions.
* Are generally metals.
* Calcium compounds are important in bones, teeth and building materials.

#### 9.3 Group 17 – Halogens

Examples: Fluorine, Chlorine, Bromine, Iodine.

Properties:

* Have seven outermost-shell electrons.
* Commonly form −1 ions.
* Are reactive non-metals.
* Reactivity generally decreases down the group.
* Many occur naturally as diatomic molecules.

Examples:

$$
H_2+Cl_2\rightarrow2HCl
$$

Hydrogen reacts with chlorine to produce hydrogen chloride.

#### 9.4 Group 18 – Noble Gases

Examples: Helium, Neon, Argon.

Properties:

* Have filled outer electron shells, with helium having two electrons.
* Have low chemical reactivity.
* Exist as individual atoms under ordinary conditions.
* Are used in lighting and other specialised applications.

### 10. Isotopes, Isobars and Isotones

These terms are important for competitive examinations.

#### 10.1 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

Example:

$$
{}^{12}_{6}C,\quad{}^{14}_{6}C
$$

Both have six protons, but their neutron counts differ.

Uses:

* Carbon-14 is used in radiocarbon dating.
* Cobalt-60 is used in certain medical and industrial applications.

#### 10.2 Isobars

Isobars are atoms of different elements with the same mass number but different atomic numbers.

Example:

$$
{}^{40}_{18}Ar,\quad{}^{40}_{20}Ca
$$

Both have mass number 40, but their atomic numbers differ.

#### 10.3 Isotones

Isotones are atoms of different elements that have the same number of neutrons.

Example:

* Carbon-14: 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons.
* Nitrogen-15: 15 − 7 = 8 neutrons.

Both have eight neutrons.

**Quick memory:**

* Isotopes: Same atomic number.
* Isobars: Same mass number.
* Isotones: Same number of neutrons.

### 11. Chemical Reactions and Formula-Based Sums

#### 11.1 Balancing Chemical Equations

A chemical equation must have the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.

**Example:**

Unbalanced equation:

$$
H_2+O_2\rightarrow H_2O
$$

Balanced equation:

$$
\boxed{2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O}
$$

Left side:

* Hydrogen atoms = 4.
* Oxygen atoms = 2.

Right side:

* Hydrogen atoms = 4.
* Oxygen atoms = 2.

The equation is balanced.

#### 11.2 Moles and Molar Mass

The mole is a unit used to express the amount of a substance.

One mole contains approximately:

$$
\boxed{6.022\times10^{23}}
$$

elementary entities. This number is called the Avogadro constant.

The formula for the number of moles is:

$$
\boxed{n=\frac{m}{M}}
$$

Where:

* \(n\) = Number of moles.
* \(m\) = Given mass in grams.
* \(M\) = Molar mass in grams per mole.

**Solved Sum 1: Number of Moles of Water**

Find the number of moles in 36 g of water.

Atomic masses:

* Hydrogen = 1.
* Oxygen = 16.

Molar mass of water:

$$
M(H_2O)=2(1)+16=18\ g/mol
$$

Number of moles:

$$
n=\frac{36}{18}
$$

$$
\boxed{n=2\ mol}
$$

**Answer: 2 moles.**

#### 11.3 Calculating Molecular Mass

Molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule.

**Solved Sum 2: Carbon Dioxide**

Calculate the molecular mass of CO₂.

Atomic masses:

* Carbon = 12.
* Oxygen = 16.

$$
M(CO_2)=12+2(16)
$$

$$
=12+32
$$

$$
\boxed{44\ u}
$$

**Answer: 44 u.**

#### 11.4 Percentage Composition

Percentage composition gives the percentage by mass of an element in a compound.

Formula:

$$
\boxed{\%\text{ of element}=
\frac{\text{Mass of the element in one mole of compound}}
{\text{Molar mass of compound}}\times100}
$$

**Solved Sum 3: Percentage of Oxygen in Water**

Molar mass of water:

$$
H_2O=18\ g/mol
$$

Mass of oxygen in one mole of water:

$$
=16\ g
$$

Therefore:

$$
\%\text{ Oxygen}=\frac{16}{18}\times100
$$

$$
\boxed{88.89\%}
$$

**Answer: Approximately 88.89%.**

#### 11.5 Density

Density is the mass per unit volume.

$$
\boxed{\rho=\frac{m}{V}}
$$

Where:

* \(\rho\) = Density.
* \(m\) = Mass.
* \(V\) = Volume.

**Solved Sum 4**

A substance has a mass of 200 g and a volume of 50 cm³. Find its density.

$$
\rho=\frac{200}{50}
$$

$$
\boxed{4\ g/cm^3}
$$

**Answer: 4 g/cm³.**

#### 11.6 Concentration – Molarity

Molarity is the number of moles of solute per litre of solution.

$$
\boxed{M=\frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution in litres}}}
$$

**Solved Sum 5**

A solution contains 0.5 mol of solute in 2 litres of solution. Calculate its molarity.

$$
M=\frac{0.5}{2}
$$

$$
\boxed{0.25\ mol/L}
$$

**Answer: 0.25 M.**

### 12. Oxidation, Reduction and Oxidation Number

Oxidation and reduction occur together in redox reactions.

**Oxidation:** Loss of electrons.

**Reduction:** Gain of electrons.

Example:

$$
Zn+CuSO_4\rightarrow ZnSO_4+Cu
$$

In this reaction:

* Zinc loses electrons and is oxidised.
* Copper ions gain electrons and are reduced.

Half-reactions:

$$
Zn\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+2e^-
$$

$$
Cu^{2+}+2e^-\rightarrow Cu
$$

**Important points:**

* Oxidation involves an increase in oxidation number.
* Reduction involves a decrease in oxidation number.
* An oxidising agent causes another substance to be oxidised and is itself reduced.
* A reducing agent causes another substance to be reduced and is itself oxidised.

### 13. Acids, Bases and pH

Acids and bases are classified according to their chemical properties.

Examples of acids:

* Hydrochloric acid — HCl.
* Sulphuric acid — H₂SO₄.
* Nitric acid — HNO₃.

Examples of bases:

* Sodium hydroxide — NaOH.
* Potassium hydroxide — KOH.
* Calcium hydroxide — Ca(OH)₂.

At approximately 25°C, for a dilute aqueous solution:

* pH below 7 indicates an acidic solution.
* pH equal to 7 indicates a neutral solution.
* pH above 7 indicates a basic solution.

The pH formula is:

$$
\boxed{pH=-\log_{10}[H^+]}
$$

Here, \([H^+]\) represents the hydrogen-ion concentration in mol/L under the usual introductory approximation.

**Solved Sum 1**

If the hydrogen-ion concentration is:

$$
[H^+]=10^{-3}\ mol/L
$$

Then:

$$
pH=-\log_{10}(10^{-3})
$$

$$
\boxed{pH=3}
$$

The solution is acidic.

**TNPSC fact:** Blue litmus turns red in an acidic solution, while red litmus turns blue in a basic solution.

### 14. Important Gas-Related Reactions

#### Preparation of Carbon Dioxide

When calcium carbonate reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid:

$$
CaCO_3+2HCl\rightarrow CaCl_2+H_2O+CO_2
$$

Carbon dioxide is released.

#### Preparation of Hydrogen

A typical reaction between zinc and dilute hydrochloric acid is:

$$
Zn+2HCl\rightarrow ZnCl_2+H_2
$$

Hydrogen gas is released.

#### Combustion of Methane

$$
CH_4+2O_2\rightarrow CO_2+2H_2O
$$

Methane burns in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water.

**Exam point:** Complete combustion of many hydrocarbons produces carbon dioxide and water.

## 18. Final Revision: Important Formulas

| Concept                | Formula                                                                   |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Atomic number          | \(Z=\text{Number of protons}\)                                            |
| Mass number            | \(A=Z+N\)                                                                 |
| Number of neutrons     | \(N=A-Z\)                                                                 |
| Maximum shell capacity | \(2n^2\)                                                                  |
| Number of moles        | \(n=m/M\)                                                                 |
| Number of particles    | \(N=nN_A\)                                                                |
| Density                | \(\rho=m/V\)                                                              |
| Molarity               | \(M=n/V\), where \(V\) is in litres                                       |
| Percentage composition | \(\frac{\text{Mass of element}}{\text{Molar mass of compound}}\times100\) |
| pH                     | \(pH=-\log_{10}[H^+]\)                                                    |

**Important:** In the molarity formula, the solution volume must be expressed in litres. In the percentage composition formula, use the mass contributed by the relevant element in one mole of the compound.
