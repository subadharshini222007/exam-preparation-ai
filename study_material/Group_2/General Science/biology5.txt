# BIOLOGY – PART 11

## ECOLOGY, ECOSYSTEM AND ENVIRONMENTAL SCIENCE

# 1. INTRODUCTION TO ECOLOGY

Ecology is the branch of biology that studies the relationships between living organisms and their environment.

The term ecology was introduced by the German zoologist Ernst Haeckel in 1866. It is derived from the Greek words “oikos,” meaning home, and “logos,” meaning study.

Ecology explains how organisms interact with one another and with non-living components such as air, water, soil, sunlight and temperature.

### Importance of Ecology

1. Helps us understand relationships among organisms.
2. Explains the flow of energy in nature.
3. Helps conserve biodiversity.
4. Supports wildlife and forest conservation.
5. Explains the effects of pollution.
6. Helps maintain ecological balance.
7. Supports sustainable use of natural resources.
8. Helps understand climate change and environmental problems.

## 1.1 Levels of Ecological Organization

Ecological organization is studied at different levels.

### 1. Organism

An organism is an individual living being.

Examples:

* A human being
* A mango tree
* A frog
* A bacterium

### 2. Population

A population is a group of individuals of the same species living in a particular area at a particular time.

Example: All deer living in a particular forest.

### 3. Community

A community consists of populations of different species living and interacting in the same area.

Example: Trees, birds, insects, fungi and mammals living in a forest.

### 4. Ecosystem

An ecosystem consists of a biological community and its physical environment interacting with each other.

Example: Pond ecosystem, forest ecosystem and grassland ecosystem.

### 5. Biome

A biome is a large geographical region characterized by its climate and typical vegetation and animals.

Examples:

* Tropical rainforest
* Desert
* Tundra
* Grassland

### 6. Biosphere

The biosphere includes all parts of Earth where life exists. It includes portions of the atmosphere, hydrosphere and lithosphere.

**Important TNPSC fact:** The biosphere is the largest level of ecological organization commonly discussed in ecology.

# 2. ENVIRONMENT

The environment includes all living and non-living factors that influence organisms.

## 2.1 Components of the Environment

### A. Biotic Components

Biotic components are the living components of an ecosystem.

Examples:

* Plants
* Animals
* Bacteria
* Fungi
* Algae

Biotic components are commonly classified as producers, consumers and decomposers.

### B. Abiotic Components

Abiotic components are the non-living physical and chemical factors of the environment.

Examples:

* Sunlight
* Temperature
* Water
* Air
* Soil
* Minerals
* Humidity
* pH
* Salinity

Abiotic factors influence the distribution, survival, growth and reproduction of organisms.

## 2.2 Habitat and Niche

### Habitat

A habitat is the natural place where an organism lives.

Examples:

* Pond: Habitat of many freshwater organisms.
* Forest: Habitat of deer, birds and trees.
* Desert: Habitat of organisms adapted to dry conditions.

### Ecological Niche

An ecological niche describes the role of an organism in its ecosystem, including how it obtains food, uses resources and interacts with other organisms.

**Difference between habitat and niche**

| Habitat                                  | Ecological Niche                               |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| The place where an organism lives        | The role played by an organism                 |
| Describes the organism's living location | Describes how the organism lives and interacts |
| Example: Pond                            | Example: An insect-eating fish in a pond       |

# 3. ECOSYSTEM

An ecosystem is a functional unit of nature in which living organisms interact with one another and with the non-living environment.

The term ecosystem was proposed by British ecologist A. G. Tansley in 1935.

## 3.1 Components of an Ecosystem

An ecosystem has two major components:

1. Abiotic components
2. Biotic components

### Abiotic Components

These include sunlight, temperature, water, air, soil and minerals.

Sunlight is the primary energy source for most ecosystems.

### Biotic Components

Biotic components are classified into the following groups.

#### 1. Producers

Producers prepare organic food from inorganic substances.

Green plants use sunlight, carbon dioxide and water during photosynthesis.

Examples:

* Grass
* Trees
* Algae
* Phytoplankton

Producers are also called autotrophs.

#### 2. Consumers

Consumers depend directly or indirectly on other organisms for food.

**Primary consumers:** Feed on producers.

Examples: Deer, rabbit, grasshopper.

**Secondary consumers:** Feed on primary consumers.

Examples: Frog feeding on grasshoppers; small carnivorous fish feeding on herbivorous organisms.

**Tertiary consumers:** Feed on secondary consumers.

Examples vary according to the food chain and ecosystem.

**Apex predators:** Occupy the highest trophic position in a particular food web and generally have few natural predators as adults.

Examples may include tigers in some forest ecosystems and large predatory fish in aquatic ecosystems.

#### 3. Decomposers

Decomposers break down dead organisms and organic wastes into simpler substances.

Examples:

* Bacteria
* Fungi

They recycle nutrients and return mineral substances to the environment.

Detritivores, such as earthworms, feed on dead organic matter. They are different from microorganisms that chemically decompose organic material.

## 3.2 Functions of an Ecosystem

The major functions of an ecosystem are:

1. Productivity
2. Energy flow
3. Decomposition
4. Nutrient cycling

### Productivity

Productivity refers to the rate at which organic matter is produced in an ecosystem.

**Gross Primary Productivity (GPP):** Total rate at which producers capture energy and produce organic matter through photosynthesis.

**Net Primary Productivity (NPP):** Organic matter remaining after producers use some of the captured energy for respiration.

Formula:

NPP = GPP − Respiration by producers

NPP represents the energy available for plant growth and for consumers that depend on producers.

### Decomposition

Decomposition is the breakdown of dead organic matter into simpler substances.

Major processes include:

* Fragmentation
* Leaching
* Catabolism
* Humification
* Mineralization

**Humification:** Formation of relatively stable organic matter called humus.

**Mineralization:** Release of inorganic nutrients from organic matter.

Decomposition is generally faster under warm, moist and oxygen-rich conditions, although the rate depends on the material and environment.

# 4. FOOD CHAIN

A food chain represents the transfer of food energy from one organism to another.

Example:

Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle

In this food chain:

* Grass is the producer.
* Grasshopper is the primary consumer.
* Frog is the secondary consumer.
* Snake is a higher-level consumer.
* Eagle occupies a higher trophic level.

## 4.1 Types of Food Chains

### A. Grazing Food Chain

Begins with living green plants.

Example:

Grass → Deer → Tiger

Another example:

Phytoplankton → Zooplankton → Small fish → Large fish

### B. Detritus Food Chain

Begins with dead organic matter.

Example:

Dead leaves → Earthworms or other detritivores → Birds

Microorganisms also play important roles in breaking down dead organic matter.

## 4.2 Food Web

A food web consists of interconnected food chains.

In natural ecosystems, most organisms have more than one food source and may be eaten by several predators.

### Importance of Food Webs

1. Demonstrate feeding relationships.
2. Provide alternative energy pathways.
3. Help explain ecosystem stability.
4. Show the interdependence of organisms.

**TNPSC point:** A food web is more complex than a single food chain because it contains multiple interconnected feeding relationships.

# 5. TROPHIC LEVELS AND ENERGY FLOW

A trophic level is the feeding position occupied by an organism in a food chain.

| Trophic Level | Organism Type       | Example     |
| ------------- | ------------------- | ----------- |
| First         | Producers           | Grass       |
| Second        | Primary consumers   | Grasshopper |
| Third         | Secondary consumers | Frog        |
| Fourth        | Tertiary consumers  | Snake       |

The exact trophic position of an organism may differ among food chains.

## 5.1 Energy Flow

Energy enters most ecosystems through sunlight.

Green plants capture solar energy through photosynthesis. This energy passes to consumers through feeding relationships.

Energy flow is generally unidirectional:

Sun → Producers → Consumers → Decomposers

Energy is lost as heat during metabolic processes at each trophic level. Therefore, the same energy cannot be recycled indefinitely.

Nutrients, in contrast, can be recycled through biogeochemical cycles.

## 5.2 Ten Percent Law

The ten percent law was proposed by ecologist Raymond Lindeman in 1942.

It states that, on average, approximately 10% of the energy at one trophic level may be transferred to the next trophic level in a simplified ecological model.

For example:

* Producers: 10,000 energy units
* Primary consumers: 1,000 energy units
* Secondary consumers: 100 energy units
* Tertiary consumers: 10 energy units

This is an illustrative model, not a fixed rule for every ecosystem. Actual transfer efficiency varies.

**Important fact:** The ten percent law is associated with Raymond Lindeman.

# 6. ECOLOGICAL PYRAMIDS

An ecological pyramid represents the relationship between trophic levels in an ecosystem.

The three major types are:

1. Pyramid of numbers
2. Pyramid of biomass
3. Pyramid of energy

## 6.1 Pyramid of Numbers

Shows the number of organisms at each trophic level.

It may be upright or inverted, depending on the ecosystem.

For example, one large tree may support many insects. In such a case, the pyramid of numbers may be inverted or irregular.

## 6.2 Pyramid of Biomass

Shows the total mass of living organisms at each trophic level, usually measured as dry mass per unit area or volume.

In many terrestrial ecosystems, the biomass pyramid is upright.

In some aquatic ecosystems, it can be inverted because phytoplankton have a small standing biomass but reproduce rapidly and support a larger biomass of consumers.

## 6.3 Pyramid of Energy

Shows the amount of energy available at each trophic level per unit area per unit time.

The energy pyramid is always upright in the conventional ecological representation because energy is lost during transfer between trophic levels.

**TNPSC quick revision:**

* Pyramid of numbers: May be upright or inverted.
* Pyramid of biomass: May be upright or inverted.
* Pyramid of energy: Always upright.

# 7. BIOGEOCHEMICAL CYCLES

Biogeochemical cycles describe the movement of chemical elements between living organisms and the physical environment.

Important cycles include:

1. Water cycle
2. Carbon cycle
3. Nitrogen cycle
4. Oxygen cycle
5. Phosphorus cycle

## 7.1 Water Cycle

The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between Earth's surface and the atmosphere.

### Major Processes

**Evaporation:** Liquid water changes into water vapour.

**Transpiration:** Plants release water vapour, mainly through stomata.

**Condensation:** Water vapour cools and forms liquid droplets.

**Precipitation:** Water falls as rain, snow or other forms.

**Infiltration:** Water enters the soil.

**Runoff:** Water flows across the land surface.

**Groundwater flow:** Water moves through underground spaces and geological formations.

### Importance

* Maintains freshwater availability.
* Supports agriculture.
* Regulates weather and climate.
* Sustains aquatic ecosystems.
* Replenishes groundwater.

## 7.2 Carbon Cycle

Carbon is essential for carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids.

### Major Processes

1. Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.
2. Carbon enters plants as organic compounds.
3. Animals obtain carbon by feeding.
4. Respiration releases carbon dioxide.
5. Decomposition returns carbon to the environment.
6. Oceans absorb and release carbon dioxide.
7. Combustion of fossil fuels releases stored carbon.

### Human Influence

Burning coal, petroleum and natural gas increases atmospheric carbon dioxide.

Deforestation reduces carbon storage in vegetation and can release stored carbon.

Higher atmospheric greenhouse-gas concentrations contribute to global warming.

## 7.3 Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is necessary for proteins, enzymes, DNA and RNA.

Although atmospheric nitrogen forms approximately 78% of Earth's atmosphere, most organisms cannot use atmospheric nitrogen gas directly.

### Major Steps

#### 1. Nitrogen Fixation

Atmospheric nitrogen is converted into biologically useful compounds.

Examples of nitrogen-fixing microorganisms include:

* Rhizobium
* Certain cyanobacteria
* Azotobacter

Rhizobium commonly forms a symbiotic association with the root nodules of leguminous plants.

Lightning can also convert atmospheric nitrogen into nitrogen compounds.

Industrial processes can produce nitrogen fertilizers.

#### 2. Nitrification

Ammonia or ammonium is converted into nitrite and then nitrate by nitrifying microorganisms.

#### 3. Assimilation

Plants absorb inorganic nitrogen compounds and incorporate nitrogen into organic molecules.

Animals obtain nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.

#### 4. Ammonification

Decomposers convert organic nitrogen from dead organisms and wastes into ammonia or ammonium compounds.

#### 5. Denitrification

Certain bacteria convert nitrate into gaseous nitrogen forms, returning nitrogen to the atmosphere.

### Importance of the Nitrogen Cycle

* Maintains nitrogen availability.
* Supports plant growth.
* Recycles nutrients.
* Helps maintain ecosystem productivity.

## 7.4 Phosphorus Cycle

Phosphorus is essential for:

* DNA and RNA
* ATP
* Phospholipids
* Bones and teeth

Weathering of phosphate-containing rocks releases phosphorus into soil and water.

Plants absorb phosphate. Animals obtain phosphorus through food. Decomposition returns phosphorus to the environment.

Unlike the carbon and nitrogen cycles, the phosphorus cycle has no major gaseous atmospheric phase under normal ecological conditions.

Excess phosphorus entering water bodies can contribute to eutrophication.

# 8. ECOLOGICAL SUCCESSION

Ecological succession is the gradual change in the species composition and structure of an ecological community over time.

## 8.1 Primary Succession

Primary succession begins in an area where soil is absent or has not developed and where a community is being established on newly exposed surfaces.

Examples:

* Newly exposed rock
* Fresh lava surfaces
* Some newly formed glacial landscapes

Lichens and other pioneer organisms may help initiate soil formation.

Primary succession is generally slow because soil and suitable conditions must develop.

## 8.2 Secondary Succession

Secondary succession occurs where a previous community has been disturbed but soil remains.

Examples:

* Abandoned agricultural land
* Forest regrowth after a fire
* Vegetation recovery after some types of storms

Secondary succession is often faster than primary succession because soil, nutrients and some biological material remain.

| Primary Succession                         | Secondary Succession            |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Begins where soil is absent or undeveloped | Begins where soil remains       |
| Usually slower                             | Often faster                    |
| Pioneer organisms help develop soil        | Existing soil supports recovery |
| Example: Newly exposed rock                | Example: Abandoned farmland     |

## 8.3 Pioneer Community

A pioneer community is the first group of organisms to colonize a developing habitat.

Examples may include lichens, mosses, grasses or other organisms, depending on environmental conditions.

## 8.4 Climax Community

In classical ecological models, a climax community is a relatively stable community that develops through succession under prevailing conditions.

Modern ecology recognizes that ecosystems are dynamic and may experience repeated disturbances rather than reaching one permanent final state.

# 9. BIODIVERSITY

Biodiversity means the variety of living organisms and the ecological systems in which they occur.

It includes variation within species, between species and among ecosystems.

## 9.1 Levels of Biodiversity

### 1. Genetic Diversity

Variation in genes within a species.

Example: Different varieties of rice with different characteristics.

Importance:

* Supports adaptation.
* Improves resilience to disease.
* Provides material for plant and animal breeding.

### 2. Species Diversity

The variety of species in a region.

Example: Different species of plants, insects, birds and mammals in a forest.

### 3. Ecosystem Diversity

The variety of ecosystems in a region.

Examples:

* Forests
* Wetlands
* Grasslands
* Deserts
* Mangroves
* Coral reefs

## 9.2 Importance of Biodiversity

1. Provides food, medicine and raw materials.
2. Supports pollination.
3. Maintains soil fertility.
4. Helps regulate climate.
5. Supports nutrient cycling.
6. Maintains ecological relationships.
7. Provides cultural, educational and recreational benefits.
8. Supports genetic resources for future use.

## 9.3 Biodiversity Hotspots

A biodiversity hotspot is a region with high concentrations of endemic species that has experienced substantial habitat loss.

The hotspot concept is associated with Norman Myers.

To qualify under the widely used Conservation International criteria, a region must contain at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species and must have lost at least 70% of its original natural vegetation.

India includes parts of four global biodiversity hotspots:

1. Himalaya
2. Indo-Burma
3. Western Ghats–Sri Lanka
4. Sundaland, represented in India by the Nicobar Islands

**TNPSC point:** The Western Ghats are an important biodiversity-rich region of southern India.

## 9.4 Endemic Species

An endemic species is naturally restricted to a particular geographical area.

Examples include species found naturally only in particular island systems or mountain ranges.

Endemism does not automatically mean that a species is endangered. However, a species restricted to a small area may be especially vulnerable to habitat destruction.

## 9.5 Threats to Biodiversity

Major threats include:

* Habitat destruction
* Habitat fragmentation
* Pollution
* Overexploitation
* Invasive alien species
* Climate change
* Illegal wildlife trade

### Habitat Destruction

Conversion of forests and wetlands into agricultural land, settlements or industrial areas can reduce available habitats.

### Invasive Alien Species

These are species introduced outside their natural range that can spread and cause ecological, economic or social harm.

Examples often discussed in Indian environmental studies include water hyacinth and Lantana camara.

### Overexploitation

Excessive hunting, fishing, logging or collection can reduce populations faster than they can recover.

# 10. CONSERVATION OF BIODIVERSITY

Biodiversity conservation aims to protect species, genetic variation and ecosystems.

## 10.1 In-Situ Conservation

In-situ conservation protects organisms in their natural habitats.

Examples:

* National parks
* Wildlife sanctuaries
* Biosphere reserves
* Community-protected areas

Advantages:

* Protects many species together.
* Maintains natural interactions.
* Allows evolutionary processes to continue.
* Can protect entire habitats.

## 10.2 Ex-Situ Conservation

Ex-situ conservation protects organisms outside their natural habitats.

Examples:

* Zoos
* Botanical gardens
* Seed banks
* Gene banks
* Tissue culture facilities
* Cryopreservation

Advantages:

* Supports breeding programmes.
* Can preserve rare genetic material.
* Helps protect species facing immediate threats.
* Supports research and education.

| In-Situ Conservation                          | Ex-Situ Conservation                            |
| --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Within the natural habitat                    | Outside the natural habitat                     |
| Protects habitats and ecological interactions | Protects selected organisms or genetic material |
| Example: National park                        | Example: Seed bank                              |
| Supports natural ecological processes         | Supports managed breeding and storage           |

## 10.3 National Park, Wildlife Sanctuary and Biosphere Reserve

### National Park

A national park is a protected area established for conservation of wildlife, habitats and ecosystems. Activities are regulated under applicable laws.

### Wildlife Sanctuary

A wildlife sanctuary is a protected area intended primarily to protect wildlife and their habitats. Specific rights and permitted activities depend on the applicable legal provisions.

### Biosphere Reserve

A biosphere reserve aims to conserve biodiversity while supporting research, education and sustainable human activities.

Biosphere reserves commonly have three zones:

1. Core zone: Strictly protected central area.
2. Buffer zone: Supports research, education and compatible activities.
3. Transition zone: Allows sustainable human activities and settlements.

# 11. ENVIRONMENTAL POLLUTION

Pollution occurs when substances or forms of energy are introduced into the environment at levels that cause harmful effects.

Major types:

* Air pollution
* Water pollution
* Soil pollution
* Noise pollution
* Thermal pollution
* Radioactive pollution
* Light pollution

## 11.1 Air Pollution

Air pollution occurs when harmful gases, particles or biological substances contaminate the atmosphere.

### Major Air Pollutants

**Particulate Matter (PM10 and PM2.5):** Small airborne particles that can enter the respiratory system. Fine particles can penetrate deeply into the lungs.

**Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂):** Produced by burning sulphur-containing fuels and certain industrial processes.

**Nitrogen Oxides (NOx):** Produced by high-temperature combustion, including vehicle engines and power plants.

**Carbon Monoxide (CO):** Formed during incomplete combustion. It reduces the blood's ability to transport oxygen.

**Ground-Level Ozone (O₃):** A secondary pollutant formed through reactions involving nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds in sunlight.

**Lead:** A toxic metal that may enter the environment from certain industrial sources and contaminated materials.

### Effects of Air Pollution

* Respiratory illness
* Cardiovascular effects
* Reduced visibility
* Damage to crops
* Damage to buildings
* Harm to wildlife
* Contribution to climate and atmospheric chemistry problems

### Control Measures

1. Improve public transport.
2. Use cleaner energy sources.
3. Maintain vehicles properly.
4. Control industrial emissions.
5. Avoid open burning of waste.
6. Increase energy efficiency.
7. Monitor air quality.
8. Protect and restore green spaces.

## 11.2 Water Pollution

Water pollution occurs when harmful substances or organisms enter water bodies and reduce water quality.

### Sources

* Untreated sewage
* Industrial effluents
* Agricultural runoff
* Oil spills
* Plastics
* Mining waste
* Pathogenic microorganisms

### Effects

* Waterborne diseases
* Fish mortality
* Reduced dissolved oxygen
* Eutrophication
* Contamination of drinking water
* Damage to aquatic biodiversity

### Water Quality Indicators

**Dissolved Oxygen (DO):** Oxygen available in water for aquatic organisms.

**Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD):** The amount of dissolved oxygen required by microorganisms to break down biodegradable organic matter under specified test conditions.

A high BOD value often indicates substantial biodegradable organic pollution.

**Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD):** An estimate of the oxygen equivalent needed to chemically oxidize substances in a water sample under a specified test.

BOD and COD are related but are not identical measurements.

## 11.3 Eutrophication

Eutrophication is the enrichment of a water body with nutrients, especially nitrogen and phosphorus.

### Causes

* Fertilizer runoff
* Sewage discharge
* Detergent-related nutrient inputs
* Agricultural drainage

### Process

1. Nutrients enter the water.
2. Algae and aquatic plants may grow rapidly.
3. Some of this biomass dies.
4. Decomposers break down the dead material.
5. Microbial decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen.
6. Oxygen depletion can harm or kill aquatic organisms.

Severe oxygen depletion may create hypoxic conditions.

## 11.4 Soil Pollution

Soil pollution occurs when harmful chemicals or wastes contaminate the soil.

Sources:

* Excessive pesticide use
* Industrial waste
* Improper disposal of batteries
* Heavy metals
* Plastic waste
* Oil contamination

Effects:

* Reduced soil fertility
* Harm to soil organisms
* Contamination of crops
* Groundwater contamination
* Entry of pollutants into food chains

Control measures include safe waste disposal, integrated pest management, soil testing and remediation of contaminated land.

## 11.5 Noise Pollution

Noise pollution refers to unwanted or excessive sound that affects human health or environmental quality.

Sources:

* Road traffic
* Aircraft
* Construction
* Industries
* Loudspeakers
* Machinery

Possible effects:

* Hearing impairment
* Sleep disturbance
* Stress
* Reduced concentration
* Communication difficulties

Noise levels are commonly measured in decibels (dB).

Control measures include noise barriers, machinery maintenance, sound insulation and appropriate regulation of loud activities.

# 12. GREENHOUSE EFFECT AND GLOBAL WARMING

## 12.1 Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect is a natural process in which certain atmospheric gases absorb and re-emit infrared radiation, helping keep Earth warm.

Major greenhouse gases include:

* Water vapour
* Carbon dioxide
* Methane
* Nitrous oxide
* Ozone
* Certain industrial gases

The natural greenhouse effect is essential for life. However, increased greenhouse-gas concentrations from human activities strengthen the effect and contribute to global warming.

## 12.2 Global Warming

Global warming refers to the long-term increase in Earth's average surface temperature, primarily driven by human-caused greenhouse-gas emissions.

Major human causes:

* Burning fossil fuels
* Deforestation
* Agriculture
* Industrial processes
* Certain waste-management practices

### Effects

1. Rising average temperatures.
2. Melting glaciers and ice sheets.
3. Rising global mean sea level.
4. Changes in rainfall patterns.
5. Increased heatwave risk.
6. Changes in ecosystem distribution.
7. Greater risks to agriculture and water security.
8. Increased stress on some wildlife populations.

## 12.3 Climate Change

Climate change includes long-term changes in temperature, rainfall, wind patterns and other climate characteristics.

Global warming is one major component of modern climate change.

### Mitigation

Mitigation means reducing greenhouse-gas emissions or increasing the removal of greenhouse gases from the atmosphere.

Examples:

* Renewable energy
* Energy efficiency
* Forest protection
* Reduced fossil-fuel use
* Low-emission transport

### Adaptation

Adaptation means adjusting systems and practices to reduce harm from actual or expected climate impacts.

Examples:

* Drought-resistant crops
* Flood-resilient infrastructure
* Heat action plans
* Water conservation
* Early-warning systems

# 13. OZONE LAYER

The ozone layer is a region of the stratosphere containing relatively high concentrations of ozone.

It absorbs much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet-B radiation.

## 13.1 Ozone Depletion

Certain chemicals, especially chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and related ozone-depleting substances, release reactive chlorine or bromine in the stratosphere.

These reactive substances participate in catalytic reactions that destroy ozone.

### Effects of Ozone Depletion

* Increased exposure to harmful ultraviolet radiation.
* Greater risk of skin damage.
* Increased risk of certain eye conditions.
* Harm to phytoplankton and some terrestrial plants.
* Potential damage to ecosystems.

## 13.2 Montreal Protocol

The Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer was adopted in 1987.

It aims to phase out the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances.

**TNPSC fact:** The Montreal Protocol is associated with protection of the ozone layer.

Do not confuse it with the Kyoto Protocol or the Paris Agreement, which are associated with climate-change action.

# 14. ACID RAIN

Acid rain refers to precipitation that is more acidic than normal because of atmospheric pollutants.

Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides can react in the atmosphere to form acids that contribute to acid deposition.

### Sources

* Coal-fired power plants
* Industrial emissions
* Vehicle emissions
* Certain natural sources

### Effects

1. Acidification of lakes and streams.
2. Damage to sensitive aquatic organisms.
3. Nutrient loss from some soils.
4. Damage to forests.
5. Corrosion of buildings and monuments.
6. Mobilization of toxic metals in certain soils.

### Control

* Reduce sulphur dioxide emissions.
* Control nitrogen oxide emissions.
* Use cleaner energy.
* Improve industrial emission controls.
* Improve energy efficiency.

# 15. SOLID WASTE MANAGEMENT

Solid waste includes unwanted materials generated by households, industries, institutions and commercial activities.

## 15.1 Types of Solid Waste

* Municipal solid waste
* Industrial waste
* Agricultural waste
* Biomedical waste
* Electronic waste
* Construction and demolition waste
* Hazardous waste

Different waste categories require different collection, treatment and disposal methods.

## 15.2 The 5Rs

Commonly used waste-reduction principles include:

1. Refuse unnecessary products.
2. Reduce consumption.
3. Reuse products.
4. Repair products where practical.
5. Recycle suitable materials.

The exact wording and order of the “Rs” can vary between educational frameworks.

## 15.3 Composting

Composting is the controlled biological breakdown of suitable organic waste into a stable soil-amendment material.

Examples of compostable materials may include vegetable scraps, fruit peels and dry leaves.

Benefits:

* Reduces biodegradable waste sent to landfill.
* Returns organic matter to soil.
* Can improve soil structure.
* Supports nutrient recycling.

## 15.4 E-Waste

Electronic waste includes discarded electrical and electronic equipment.

Examples:

* Computers
* Mobile phones
* Printers
* Televisions
* Circuit boards

E-waste may contain recoverable valuable materials as well as hazardous substances.

It should be collected and processed through appropriate authorized systems rather than burned or dumped carelessly.

# 16. RENEWABLE AND NON-RENEWABLE RESOURCES

## 16.1 Renewable Resources

Renewable resources are replenished naturally on a human-relevant timescale when managed appropriately.

Examples:

* Solar energy
* Wind energy
* Hydropower
* Sustainable biomass
* Geothermal energy

Renewable resources can still cause environmental impacts if poorly planned or managed.

## 16.2 Non-Renewable Resources

These resources are finite or form much more slowly than humans consume them.

Examples:

* Coal
* Petroleum
* Natural gas
* Many mineral resources

### Conservation Measures

1. Reduce unnecessary energy consumption.
2. Improve energy efficiency.
3. Use renewable energy where suitable.
4. Reduce material waste.
5. Reuse and recycle products.
6. Protect forests and watersheds.
7. Use water efficiently.

# 17. FOREST CONSERVATION

Forests provide habitat, store carbon, regulate water flow and protect soil.

### Importance of Forests

* Maintain biodiversity.
* Reduce soil erosion.
* Support the water cycle.
* Store carbon.
* Provide timber and non-timber products.
* Support livelihoods.
* Help moderate local environmental conditions.

### Deforestation

Deforestation is the removal of forest cover and conversion of forest land to other uses.

Causes include:

* Agricultural expansion
* Infrastructure development
* Mining
* Urban expansion
* Unsustainable logging
* Some types of forest fires

Effects include habitat loss, soil erosion, biodiversity decline and changes in local water cycles.

### Afforestation and Reforestation

**Afforestation:** Establishing forest on land that has not recently been forested, according to the definition used in a particular context.

**Reforestation:** Re-establishing forest on land where forest was previously present.

Planting trees alone does not automatically restore a complete natural forest ecosystem. Species selection, soil conditions, water availability and biodiversity also matter.

# 18. ENVIRONMENTAL PROTECTION IN INDIA

India has laws and institutions designed to protect the environment.

Important laws include:

### Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972

Provides a legal framework for the protection of specified wild animals, plants and habitats, with provisions amended over time.

### Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974

A major law addressing prevention and control of water pollution.

### Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980

Originally enacted to regulate the diversion of forest land for non-forest purposes. The law's current name and scope should be studied using the latest official syllabus and legal materials because amendments have changed aspects of the framework.

### Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

Addresses prevention, control and reduction of air pollution.

# 1. NATURAL RESOURCES

Natural resources are materials, organisms and forms of energy obtained from nature and used to support life and human activities.

Examples:

* Air
* Water
* Soil
* Forests
* Wildlife
* Minerals
* Sunlight
* Wind
* Fossil fuels

Natural resources support agriculture, industries, transport, energy production and human settlements.

## 1.1 Classification of Natural Resources

Natural resources can be classified in several ways.

### A. Renewable Resources

Renewable resources are replenished naturally over time, although the rate of replenishment varies.

Examples:

* Solar energy
* Wind energy
* Flowing water
* Sustainably managed forests
* Certain biological resources

A renewable resource can still be depleted if it is consumed faster than it is replenished.

### B. Non-Renewable Resources

These are resources that form extremely slowly compared with the rate at which humans generally use them.

Examples:

* Coal
* Petroleum
* Natural gas
* Many mineral deposits

### C. Biotic Resources

Resources obtained from living organisms or biological systems.

Examples:

* Forest products
* Fish
* Livestock
* Agricultural crops

### D. Abiotic Resources

Resources derived from non-living components of nature.

Examples:

* Air
* Water
* Rocks
* Minerals
* Sunlight

### E. Exhaustible and Inexhaustible Resources

Exhaustible resources are limited or can be depleted through use.

Examples: Coal, petroleum and many mineral deposits.

Inexhaustible resources are available in very large quantities relative to human use, or are continuously supplied.

Examples often include sunlight and wind.

However, the infrastructure used to capture these resources may require land, materials and maintenance.

# 2. WATER RESOURCES

Water is essential for all known forms of life. It is required for cellular reactions, transport of substances, temperature regulation and many ecological processes.

## 2.1 Distribution of Water on Earth

Most of Earth's water is saline and occurs in oceans. Only a small proportion is freshwater, and much freshwater is stored in glaciers, ice sheets and groundwater.

The proportion of readily accessible freshwater in rivers and lakes is very small compared with total global water.

This is why freshwater management is important despite the apparent abundance of water on Earth.

## 2.2 Uses of Water

1. Drinking
2. Cooking
3. Agriculture
4. Industrial production
5. Electricity generation
6. Sanitation
7. Transportation
8. Maintenance of aquatic ecosystems

Agriculture is a major water user in many countries, including India.

## 2.3 Water Scarcity

Water scarcity occurs when available water resources are insufficient in quantity or quality to meet demand.

### Causes

* Irregular rainfall
* Drought
* Over-extraction of groundwater
* Population growth
* Urbanization
* Pollution
* Inefficient irrigation
* Loss of wetlands
* Unequal distribution of water

### Effects

* Reduced crop production
* Drinking-water shortages
* Damage to aquatic ecosystems
* Economic difficulties
* Increased competition among water users
* Greater risk of water-related conflict

## 2.4 Groundwater

Groundwater is water stored beneath Earth's surface in soil pores, fractures and permeable geological formations.

An aquifer is a geological formation that can store and transmit usable quantities of groundwater.

### Importance

* Provides drinking water.
* Supports agriculture.
* Maintains water availability during dry periods.
* Can sustain springs and streams.

### Problems Associated with Overuse

* Falling groundwater levels
* Increased pumping costs
* Drying of some wells
* Land subsidence in susceptible areas
* Saltwater intrusion in some coastal aquifers
* Reduced groundwater contribution to rivers and wetlands

## 2.5 Rainwater Harvesting

Rainwater harvesting is the collection and storage of rainwater for later use or groundwater recharge.

### Methods

* Rooftop rainwater harvesting
* Storage tanks
* Recharge pits
* Recharge wells
* Percolation ponds
* Check dams
* Contour bunding

### Benefits

1. Reduces runoff.
2. Supports groundwater recharge where conditions are suitable.
3. Reduces pressure on conventional water supplies.
4. Helps manage rainfall variability.
5. Can reduce local flooding when designed appropriately.

### Rainwater Harvesting in Tamil Nadu

Rainwater harvesting has been promoted in Tamil Nadu as a method of improving water conservation and groundwater recharge.

Its effectiveness depends on rainfall, soil permeability, local geology, design and maintenance.

## 2.6 Watershed Management

A watershed is an area of land from which water drains toward a common outlet.

Watershed management involves coordinated practices to conserve soil, water and vegetation across the drainage area.

Methods include:

* Check dams
* Contour bunding
* Vegetative barriers
* Afforestation using suitable native species
* Restoration of tanks and ponds
* Protection of drainage channels
* Soil-moisture conservation

# 3. SOIL RESOURCES

Soil is a mixture of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air and living organisms.

It supports plant growth and provides habitat for many organisms.

## 3.1 Importance of Soil

* Supports agriculture.
* Stores and filters water.
* Recycles nutrients.
* Provides habitat for soil organisms.
* Stores carbon.
* Supports natural vegetation.
* Helps stabilize terrestrial ecosystems.

## 3.2 Soil Formation

Soil formation is influenced by:

1. Parent material
2. Climate
3. Organisms
4. Relief or topography
5. Time

Weathering breaks rocks into smaller particles. Biological activity and the accumulation of organic matter help develop soil properties.

## 3.3 Soil Horizons

A typical soil profile may contain several horizons.

**O Horizon:** Surface organic layer containing litter and decomposing organic matter.

**A Horizon:** Topsoil, often containing mineral particles and organic matter.

**E Horizon:** A zone of eluviation, where certain materials may be washed out. It is not present in every soil.

**B Horizon:** Subsoil where materials transported from upper layers may accumulate.

**C Horizon:** Partly weathered parent material.

**R Horizon:** Relatively unweathered bedrock.

Not all soils have every horizon clearly developed.

## 3.4 Soil Erosion

Soil erosion is the removal of soil particles by water, wind or other forces.

### Causes

* Deforestation
* Overgrazing
* Poor agricultural practices
* Construction
* Mining
* Removal of vegetation
* Heavy rainfall on exposed soil

### Effects

* Loss of fertile topsoil
* Reduced agricultural productivity
* Sedimentation of rivers and reservoirs
* Increased runoff
* Damage to aquatic habitats

## 3.5 Soil Conservation

Methods include:

### Contour Ploughing

Ploughing along contour lines to reduce the speed of runoff on slopes.

### Terracing

Creating step-like surfaces on suitable slopes to reduce erosion and improve water retention.

### Strip Cropping

Growing crops in alternating strips to reduce erosion.

### Shelter Belts

Planting rows of trees or shrubs to reduce wind speed and wind erosion.

### Cover Crops

Growing plants to protect soil between main cropping periods.

### Afforestation and Vegetative Restoration

Maintaining suitable plant cover can reduce erosion and improve soil structure.

# 11. BIOMAGNIFICATION AND BIOACCUMULATION

## 11.1 Bioaccumulation

Bioaccumulation is the buildup of a substance within an organism over time when absorption exceeds elimination.

## 11.2 Biomagnification

Biomagnification is the increase in the concentration of certain persistent substances at higher trophic levels in a food chain.

It is especially important for substances that persist in the environment and accumulate in organisms.

Certain forms of mercury and some persistent organic pollutants can biomagnify.

### Example

A persistent pollutant enters an aquatic ecosystem.

Small organisms absorb it. Small fish eat these organisms, and larger fish eat the smaller fish.

If the pollutant accumulates and is not readily eliminated, concentrations may become higher in organisms at higher trophic levels.

### Difference

| Bioaccumulation                       | Biomagnification                                        |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Buildup within one organism over time | Increase in concentration across trophic levels         |
| Focuses on an individual organism     | Focuses on feeding relationships                        |
| May occur without biomagnification    | Depends on pollutant properties and ecological transfer |

# 16. ENVIRONMENTAL MOVEMENTS IN INDIA

The Chipko Movement is associated with forest conservation in the Himalayan region, particularly in present-day Uttarakhand.

Community members used non-violent methods, including embracing trees, to oppose certain tree-felling activities.

Important names associated with the movement include Gaura Devi, Chandi Prasad Bhatt and Sunderlal Bahuguna, in different roles and phases.

## 16.2 Appiko Movement

The Appiko Movement emerged in Karnataka in 1983 and was inspired in part by the Chipko Movement.

It focused on forest protection and sustainable use in the Western Ghats.

Pandurang Hegde is closely associated with the movement.

## 16.3 Silent Valley Movement

The Silent Valley Movement in Kerala opposed a proposed hydroelectric project that threatened a biologically important tropical evergreen forest.

The campaign contributed to the protection of Silent Valley.

## 16.4 Narmada Bachao Andolan

The Narmada Bachao Andolan raised concerns about the social and environmental consequences of large dam projects in the Narmada Valley, including displacement and rehabilitation.

Medha Patkar is a prominent figure associated with the movement.

# 18. CARBON FOOTPRINT AND ECOLOGICAL FOOTPRINT

## 18.1 Carbon Footprint

A carbon footprint estimates greenhouse-gas emissions associated with an activity, product, organization or individual, usually expressed as carbon dioxide equivalents.

Sources may include:

* Electricity use
* Transport
* Heating and cooling
* Food production
* Manufacturing
* Waste disposal

Carbon footprints depend on the boundaries and methods used in the calculation.

## 18.2 Ecological Footprint

An ecological footprint estimates the biologically productive land and water area needed to provide the resources consumed and absorb certain wastes generated by a population or activity, using a specified accounting framework.

It is broader than a carbon footprint.

| Carbon Footprint                               | Ecological Footprint                                    |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Focuses on greenhouse-gas emissions            | Focuses on resource demand and ecological capacity      |
| Commonly expressed as CO₂-equivalent emissions | Commonly expressed in standardized area units           |
| Used in climate-related assessments            | Used in broader resource and sustainability assessments |

# 19. CIRCULAR ECONOMY

A circular economy aims to reduce waste and keep products, materials and resources in use for as long as practical.

It contrasts with a simple linear model:

Take → Make → Use → Dispose

A circular approach may emphasize:

Reduce → Reuse → Repair → Refurbish → Remanufacture → Recycle

### Benefits

* Reduces waste.
* Conserves materials.
* Can reduce demand for virgin resources.
* Encourages product durability.
* Supports resource efficiency.
* May reduce some environmental impacts.

Recycling is useful, but preventing unnecessary material use and extending product life can also be important.

# 20. GREEN TECHNOLOGY

Green technology refers to technologies and practices intended to reduce environmental harm or improve resource efficiency.

Examples:

* Renewable-energy systems
* Energy-efficient lighting
* Electric public transport
* Wastewater treatment
* Precision irrigation
* Building energy management
* Pollution-control equipment
* Battery recycling systems

The overall environmental benefit depends on the full life cycle, including materials, manufacturing, operation and disposal.

# 21. ENVIRONMENTAL PROBLEMS IN TAMIL NADU: GENERAL STUDY NOTES

Tamil Nadu contains coastal ecosystems, rivers, wetlands, agricultural landscapes, forests, hill ecosystems and urban environments.

Environmental issues vary by location and change over time.

Important areas for study include:

### Water Management

Rainfall variability, groundwater use, tank restoration and water conservation are important in many districts.

### Coastal Ecosystems

Coastal areas may face erosion, flooding, pollution and habitat pressure. Mangroves and wetlands can provide important ecological services.

### Urban Pollution

Urban areas may experience traffic emissions, wastewater challenges, solid-waste problems and pressure on green spaces.

### Forest and Wildlife Conservation

The Western Ghats and other forested regions support biodiversity and require habitat protection.

### Waste Management

Segregation, recycling, composting, safe disposal and wastewater treatment are important parts of local environmental management.

For examination preparation, study current official information about Tamil Nadu's protected areas, wetlands, rivers, environmental institutions and conservation programmes.
