TOPIC: MEASUREMENT, PHYSICAL QUANTITIES AND UNITS

=================================================

SUBHEADING: PHYSICS AND SCIENTIFIC METHOD

Physics studies matter, energy, motion, forces and the interactions that govern natural phenomena. A
scientific explanation is built from observation, measurement, hypothesis, controlled testing and
analysis. A hypothesis is testable; a theory is a broad explanation supported by substantial
evidence; a law describes a consistent relationship under specified conditions. A scientific law
does not necessarily explain why a relationship occurs, and a theory does not simply become a law
after enough time.

SUBHEADING: PHYSICAL QUANTITIES

A physical quantity is a measurable property expressed as a numerical value and a unit. Fundamental
quantities are defined independently within a system of units, while derived quantities are formed
from combinations of fundamental quantities. Length, mass, time, electric current, thermodynamic
temperature, amount of substance and luminous intensity are the seven SI base quantities. Area,
volume, speed, acceleration, force and density are derived quantities.

SUBHEADING: DIMENSIONS AND DIMENSIONAL FORMULAE

Dimensions express a derived quantity in terms of fundamental dimensions such as mass [M], length

TOPIC: MOTION, SPEED, VELOCITY AND ACCELERATION

===============================================



SUBHEADING: REST AND MOTION ARE RELATIVE

An object is at rest or in motion relative to a chosen reference frame. A passenger sitting in a
moving bus is at rest relative to the seat but moving relative to the road. Motion descriptions
require a reference point and time interval. This idea is important in conceptual questions that try
to treat rest as an absolute condition.

SUBHEADING: DISTANCE AND DISPLACEMENT

Distance is the total length of the actual path travelled and is a scalar. Displacement is the
directed change from initial to final position and is a vector. Distance is never negative and is at
least as large as the magnitude of displacement. After a complete round trip, displacement can be
zero even though distance is positive.

SUBHEADING: SPEED AND AVERAGE SPEED

Average speed equals total distance divided by total time. Instantaneous speed describes the rate of
motion at a particular instant. If a journey has several parts, average speed must be calculated
using the total distance and total time, not by taking a simple arithmetic average of the separate
speeds unless the time intervals make that valid.

SUBHEADING: VELOCITY

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement and includes direction. Average velocity is total
displacement divided by total time. An object moving around a circular track can have a changing
velocity even when its speed is constant, because direction changes continuously. Velocity may be
positive, negative or zero depending on the chosen axis and motion.

SUBHEADING: ACCELERATION

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity with time. It can arise from a change in speed,
direction or both. Uniform acceleration means equal changes in velocity in equal time intervals.
Deceleration is acceleration opposite to the direction of velocity in common one-dimensional
situations; the sign of acceleration itself depends on the coordinate convention.

SUBHEADING: EQUATIONS OF UNIFORMLY ACCELERATED MOTION

For straight-line motion with constant acceleration, v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; and v² = u² + 2as.
Here u is initial velocity, v final velocity, a acceleration, t time and s displacement. These
equations should not be applied unchanged when acceleration varies significantly with time. Use SI
units consistently: metres, seconds and metres per second squared.

SUBHEADING: DISTANCE-TIME AND VELOCITY-TIME GRAPHS

The slope of a distance-time graph gives speed when the graph represents distance travelled. The
slope of a displacement-time graph gives velocity. The slope of a velocity-time graph gives
acceleration, and the signed area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement. The area under a
speed-time graph gives distance travelled. Graph interpretation is often more important than
memorising the formula alone.

SUBHEADING: CIRCULAR MOTION

In circular motion, the direction of velocity changes continually. Centripetal acceleration points
toward the centre and has magnitude v²/r for speed v and radius r. A centripetal force is not a new
type of force; it is the net inward force supplied by tension, friction, gravity or another
interaction. If the inward net force disappears, an object continues along its instantaneous tangent
rather than flying outward because of a separate outward force.

TOPIC: FORCE, NEWTON'S LAWS AND MOMENTUM

========================================



SUBHEADING: FORCE AND ITS EFFECTS

A force is an interaction that can change an object's state of motion, direction or shape. Force is
a vector and its SI unit is the newton (N). The net force is the vector sum of all forces acting on
an object. Balanced forces produce zero net force and no acceleration, though they may still deform
an object or maintain motion at constant velocity.

SUBHEADING: NEWTON'S FIRST LAW AND INERTIA

Newton's first law states that an object remains at rest or continues in uniform straight-line
motion unless acted on by a net external force. Inertia is the tendency to resist changes in motion.
Mass is a measure of inertia. Passengers lurch forward when a moving vehicle stops because their
bodies tend to maintain their previous motion; seat belts provide the force needed to change that
motion safely.

SUBHEADING: NEWTON'S SECOND LAW

The net force equals the rate of change of momentum. For constant mass, F = ma. One newton is the
force that gives a one-kilogram mass an acceleration of one metre per second squared. For the same
net force, a larger mass experiences a smaller acceleration. For the same mass, a larger net force
produces a larger acceleration.

SUBHEADING: NEWTON'S THIRD LAW

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The two forces act on different bodies, so
they do not cancel each other on a single free-body diagram. A swimmer pushes water backward and the
water pushes the swimmer forward. A rocket accelerates by expelling gases backward. Walking relies
on the interaction between the foot and the ground.

SUBHEADING: MOMENTUM AND IMPULSE

Momentum is mass multiplied by velocity, p = mv, and is a vector. Impulse equals the change in
momentum and also equals force multiplied by the time interval when force is constant. Increasing
the time over which momentum changes reduces the average force for the same momentum change. Airbags
and cushioning materials increase stopping time and reduce injury risk in collisions.

SUBHEADING: CONSERVATION OF MOMENTUM

In an isolated system with negligible net external force, total momentum remains constant. During a
collision, the momenta of interacting objects may change individually while their total remains
constant. The principle applies to recoil, explosions and many collision problems. Kinetic energy,
unlike momentum, is not conserved in every collision; in an inelastic collision some kinetic energy
becomes internal energy, sound or deformation.

SUBHEADING: FRICTION

Friction opposes relative motion or the tendency for relative motion between contacting surfaces.
Static friction adjusts up to a maximum value; kinetic friction acts when surfaces slide. Friction
provides grip for walking and braking, but it also causes wear and heat. Lubrication, ball bearings
and streamlining reduce different kinds of resistance. Friction is not always undesirable.

SUBHEADING: MOMENTUM AND SAFETY APPLICATIONS

A short stopping time creates a large average force for a given momentum change. Safety design
therefore attempts to lengthen the time or distance over which a person or vehicle slows down.
Crumple zones deform to absorb energy; helmets spread and cushion impact; seat belts restrain
occupants. These applications follow mechanics principles rather than eliminating the underlying
momentum change.

TOPIC: WORK, ENERGY AND POWER

=============================



SUBHEADING: WORK IN PHYSICS

In mechanics, work is done when a force produces displacement. For a constant force, W = Fs cos θ,
where θ is the angle between force and displacement. Work is positive when the force has a component
along displacement, negative when it opposes displacement, and zero when force is perpendicular to
displacement or when there is no displacement. Holding a heavy bag stationary involves effort
biologically but zero mechanical work on the bag in the simple physics definition.

SUBHEADING: KINETIC ENERGY

Kinetic energy is the energy of motion and is given by KE = ½mv². It depends linearly on mass and on
the square of speed. Doubling speed makes kinetic energy four times as large for the same mass. This
square dependence explains why high-speed collisions can be much more damaging than low-speed
collisions.

SUBHEADING: POTENTIAL ENERGY

Gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface can be expressed as PE = mgh relative to a
chosen reference level. It depends on mass, gravitational acceleration and height. The reference
level may be chosen for convenience; changes in potential energy are physically meaningful. Elastic
potential energy can be stored in stretched or compressed elastic objects.

SUBHEADING: CONSERVATION AND TRANSFORMATION OF ENERGY

Energy can transfer between objects and change form. In an ideal isolated system, total energy is
conserved. A falling object converts gravitational potential energy into kinetic energy; a generator
converts mechanical energy into electrical energy; a motor converts electrical energy into
mechanical energy. In real processes, some energy is transferred to the surroundings as thermal
energy or sound, making useful energy less available even though total energy remains conserved.

SUBHEADING: POWER AND EFFICIENCY

Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy: P = W/t. The SI unit is the watt, equal to
one joule per second. Efficiency is useful output energy divided by input energy, multiplied by 100%
when expressed as a percentage. Real machines have efficiency below 100% because of friction,
heating, sound and other losses.

SUBHEADING: SIMPLE MACHINES

Levers, pulleys, inclined planes, wheels and axles, screws and wedges can change the magnitude or

machine, work input equals work output; real machines require more input work than useful output
work. A machine does not create energy, but it can make a task more convenient.

TOPIC: GRAVITATION AND SATELLITES

=================================



SUBHEADING: UNIVERSAL LAW OF GRAVITATION

Every pair of masses attracts one another. The magnitude of the force is F = Gm₁m₂/r², where G is
the universal gravitational constant and r is the separation between the centres of the masses. The
force increases with either mass and decreases with the square of separation. Gravity governs
falling objects, planetary orbits, tides and satellite motion.

SUBHEADING: MASS AND WEIGHT

Mass measures inertia and is expressed in kilograms; weight is the gravitational force on a body, W
= mg, and is expressed in newtons. Mass does not ordinarily change with location, while weight
varies with gravitational field strength. An astronaut in orbit experiences apparent weightlessness
because the astronaut and spacecraft are in continuous free fall together, not because Earth's
gravity has vanished.

SUBHEADING: VARIATION OF G

The acceleration due to gravity varies with altitude, depth, latitude and local geology. In a
simplified spherical model, gravity decreases with altitude as distance from Earth's centre
increases. Inside an ideal uniform-density sphere, gravitational acceleration decreases toward zero
at the centre. Earth's rotation and non-spherical shape also contribute to differences in effective
gravity.

SUBHEADING: FREE FALL

Free fall is motion under gravity alone, neglecting air resistance. In a vacuum, objects released
from the same point have the same gravitational acceleration regardless of mass. In air, shape and
drag can make their observed motion different. A feather and a coin fall together in a vacuum but
not necessarily in ordinary air.

SUBHEADING: ESCAPE SPEED AND ORBITAL MOTION

Escape speed is the minimum launch speed needed to escape a body's gravitational field without
further propulsion, under an idealised model. An orbiting satellite is continuously falling toward
Earth while moving sideways fast enough that the curved surface falls away beneath it. Orbital speed
depends on the central mass and orbital radius, not simply on the satellite's own mass.

SUBHEADING: GEOSTATIONARY SATELLITES

A geostationary satellite orbits above the equator in the same direction as Earth's rotation and has
an orbital period equal to one sidereal day. It appears nearly fixed above one longitude and is
useful for communications and continuous weather observation. A satellite with a 24-hour period in a
tilted or non-circular orbit is not necessarily geostationary.

SUBHEADING: TIDES

Tides result primarily from the differential gravitational effects of the Moon and Sun across Earth,
together with Earth's rotation and basin geometry. Spring tides occur when the Sun, Moon and Earth
are aligned near new or full moon; neap tides occur near the first and third quarter phases. Local
coast shape and ocean depth influence the actual tidal range.

TOPIC: PROPERTIES OF MATTER: DENSITY, PRESSURE AND ELASTICITY

SUBHEADING: STATES OF MATTER AND PARTICLE MODEL

Solids generally maintain shape and volume, liquids maintain volume but take the shape of their
container, and gases expand to fill available space. These macroscopic properties arise from
particle spacing, motion and intermolecular interactions. Heating usually increases average particle
kinetic energy, though phase changes can absorb energy without a temperature rise while the change
is occurring.

SUBHEADING: DENSITY AND RELATIVE DENSITY

Density is mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V. SI density is measured in kg/m³. Relative density is the
ratio of a material's density to a reference density and has no unit. Density can help identify
materials and explain floating, but the behaviour of an object in a fluid depends on the object's
average density, shape, trapped air and the fluid's density.

SUBHEADING: PRESSURE IN SOLIDS AND FLUIDS

Pressure is normal force per unit area, P = F/A, measured in pascals (Pa). For the same force, a
smaller contact area creates greater pressure. Fluid pressure increases with depth according to P =
ρgh for a stationary fluid of uniform density, relative to the surface pressure. At the same depth
in a connected stationary fluid, pressure is equal when the fluid and conditions are the same.

SUBHEADING: PASCAL'S PRINCIPLE

A pressure change applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted throughout the fluid. Hydraulic lifts
and hydraulic brakes use this principle. A small input force applied to a small piston can produce a
larger output force on a larger piston, but the output piston moves a smaller distance in an ideal
system. The system does not multiply work or create energy.

SUBHEADING: ARCHIMEDES' PRINCIPLE AND BUOYANCY

An immersed object experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced.
An object floats when buoyancy balances its weight. If its average density is lower than that of the
fluid, it can float with only part of its volume submerged. A steel ship can float because its
hollow shape makes the average density of the whole ship less than that of water.

SUBHEADING: SURFACE TENSION AND CAPILLARITY

Surface tension arises from intermolecular forces at a liquid surface and makes the surface behave
as if it were stretched. Small droplets tend toward shapes with lower surface area. Capillary rise
or depression occurs because adhesive forces between liquid and surface interact with cohesive
forces within the liquid. Thin tubes, porous materials and plant xylem can show capillary effects.

SUBHEADING: VISCOSITY

Viscosity describes a fluid's resistance to flow. Honey has greater viscosity than water under
ordinary conditions. Viscosity generally decreases with temperature for many liquids but increases
with temperature for gases. Flow depends on viscosity, pressure differences, tube dimensions and
whether flow is laminar or turbulent.

SUBHEADING: ELASTICITY AND STRESS-STRAIN

Elasticity is the tendency of a material to return to its original shape after the deforming force
is removed, within its elastic limit. Stress is force per unit area; strain is fractional
deformation and has no unit. Hooke's law states that stress is proportional to strain within the
proportional limit for suitable materials. Beyond the elastic limit, permanent deformation may
occur.

lifting devices, bells and many electric machines.

SUBHEADING: FORCE ON A CURRENT-CARRYING CONDUCTOR

SUBHEADING: ELECTROMAGNETIC INDUCTION

A changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force. The change may be caused
by relative motion, a changing field or a changing loop area. Faraday's law relates induced emf to

produces it. Induction is the principle behind generators and transformers.

SUBHEADING: ELECTRIC MOTORS AND GENERATORS

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy using forces on current-carrying
conductors. A generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by electromagnetic
induction. A transformer changes AC voltage using mutual induction between coils and a changing
magnetic field. A conventional transformer does not operate on steady DC in the same way because the
magnetic flux must change.

SUBHEADING: DOPING AND P-N JUNCTIONS

Doping introduces small quantities of selected impurities to control charge-carrier concentration.

SUBHEADING: STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM

An atom contains a small, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons. The nucleus contains protons and
neutrons, collectively called nucleons. Atomic number is the number of protons; mass number is the
total number of protons and neutrons. Neutral atoms have equal numbers of protons and electrons.
Isotopes have the same atomic number but different numbers of neutrons.

SUBHEADING: NUCLEAR FORCES AND STABILITY

The strong nuclear interaction binds nucleons over very short distances and helps overcome
electrostatic repulsion between protons. Nuclear stability depends on the balance of nuclear forces,
proton-neutron ratio and binding energy. Some nuclei are unstable and transform spontaneously,
emitting radiation. Radioactive decay is statistical for a large collection of nuclei and cannot be
predicted exactly for one nucleus at one moment.

SUBHEADING: ALPHA, BETA AND GAMMA RADIATION

Alpha radiation consists of helium nuclei and has relatively low penetration but strong ionising
ability. Beta radiation consists of energetic electrons or positrons and has intermediate
penetration depending on energy and material. Gamma radiation is high-energy electromagnetic
radiation and is highly penetrating. Shielding choice depends on radiation type and energy; safety
requires time, distance, shielding and controlled procedures.

SUBHEADING: HALF-LIFE

Half-life is the time required for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. After each
half-life, the remaining amount is halved: one half remains after one half-life, one quarter after
two, one eighth after three. Half-life is characteristic of a particular radionuclide and is not
generally changed by ordinary temperature or pressure conditions.

SUBHEADING: NUCLEAR FISSION

Fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into lighter nuclei, often releasing neutrons and
energy. A chain reaction occurs when emitted neutrons trigger further fissions. Reactor designs
control the chain reaction and remove heat; shielding and safety systems are essential. Energy
released comes from a change in nuclear binding energy and mass-energy equivalence.

SUBHEADING: MASS-ENERGY EQUIVALENCE

Einstein's relation E = mc² expresses the equivalence of mass and energy. A small mass difference
can correspond to a large energy because c² is very large. In nuclear reactions, the mass of

energy. The relation does not mean ordinary mass disappears without conservation accounting.

SUBHEADING: RADIATION IN MEDICINE AND INDUSTRY

Radiation has beneficial uses in diagnosis, cancer treatment, sterilisation and industrial
