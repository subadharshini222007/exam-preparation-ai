1. PHYSICAL QUANTITIES, SI UNITS AND DIMENSIONS
============================================================

1.1 Fundamental SI quantities
- Length: metre (m), dimension [L]
- Mass: kilogram (kg), dimension [M]
- Time: second (s), dimension [T]
- Electric current: ampere (A)
- Thermodynamic temperature: kelvin (K)
- Amount of substance: mole (mol)
- Luminous intensity: candela (cd)

1.2 Common derived quantities
- Area = length × breadth; unit m²; dimension [L²]
- Volume = length × breadth × height; unit m³; dimension [L³]
- Speed/velocity = displacement or distance per time; unit m s⁻¹; dimension [LT⁻¹]
- Acceleration = change in velocity / time; unit m s⁻²; dimension [LT⁻²]
- Force = mass × acceleration; unit newton (N); dimension [MLT⁻²]
- Momentum = mass × velocity; unit kg m s⁻¹; dimension [MLT⁻¹]
- Work/energy = force × displacement; unit joule (J); dimension [ML²T⁻²]
- Power = work / time; unit watt (W); dimension [ML²T⁻³]
- Pressure = force / area; unit pascal (Pa); dimension [ML⁻¹T⁻²]
- Density = mass / volume; unit kg m⁻³; dimension [ML⁻³]
- Frequency = number of oscillations / time; unit hertz (Hz); dimension [T⁻¹]
- Electric charge = current × time; unit coulomb (C)
- Potential difference = work / charge; unit volt (V)
- Resistance = potential difference / current; unit ohm (Ω)
- Power of a lens = 1 / focal length in metres; unit dioptre (D)

1.3 Prefixes to remember
- kilo (k) = 10³
- mega (M) = 10⁶
- giga (G) = 10⁹
- centi (c) = 10⁻²
- milli (m) = 10⁻³
- micro (µ) = 10⁻⁶
- nano (n) = 10⁻⁹

1.4 Dimensional checks
An equation must be dimensionally consistent: the dimensions on the left and right sides must match. Dimensional analysis can check consistency, but it cannot determine a dimensionless numerical constant or prove that an equation is physically correct in every respect.

2. MOTION AND KINEMATICS
============================================================

2.1 Basic definitions
- Distance: total path length; scalar.
- Displacement: directed change from initial to final position; vector.
- Speed = distance / time.
- Average speed = total distance / total time.
- Velocity = displacement / time.
- Average velocity = total displacement / total time.
- Acceleration = change in velocity / time.

2.2 Uniform acceleration equations
For straight-line motion with constant acceleration:
1. v = u + at
2. s = ut + ½at²
3. v² = u² + 2as
4. s = (u + v)t / 2

Symbols:
u = initial velocity (m/s)
v = final velocity (m/s)
a = acceleration (m/s²)
t = time (s)
s = displacement (m)

These equations are not generally valid when acceleration changes unpredictably with time.

2.3 Graphs
- Slope of a displacement–time graph = velocity.
- Slope of a velocity–time graph = acceleration.
- Area under a velocity–time graph = displacement.
- Area under a speed–time graph = distance travelled (for non-negative speed).

2.4 Circular motion
- Frequency: f = number of revolutions / time.
- Time period: T = 1/f.
- Angular speed: ω = 2π/T = 2πf.
- Linear speed: v = rω.
- Centripetal acceleration: a_c = v²/r = rω².
- Centripetal force: F_c = mv²/r = mrω².
The centripetal force is directed towards the centre of the circular path.

2.5 Relative speed
- Same direction: relative speed = difference between speeds.
- Opposite directions: relative speed = sum of speeds.
Use consistent units before calculating.

3. NEWTON’S LAWS, FORCE AND MOMENTUM
============================================================

3.1 Newton’s First Law (law of inertia)
An object remains at rest or continues in uniform straight-line motion unless acted upon by a net external force. Inertia is the tendency to resist a change in motion. Mass is a measure of inertia.

3.2 Newton’s Second Law
Net force equals the rate of change of momentum. For constant mass:
F = ma
Impulse J = FΔt (for constant force)
More generally, impulse = change in momentum:
J = Δp = mv − mu
Unit of impulse: N s, equivalent to kg m s⁻¹.

3.3 Newton’s Third Law
For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The two forces act on different bodies, so they do not cancel each other on a single-body free-body diagram.

3.4 Momentum
p = mv
Conservation of momentum: if the net external force on a system is zero, total momentum remains constant.
For two bodies in a one-dimensional interaction:
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
Choose a sign convention for directions.

3.5 Friction
- Friction acts opposite to relative motion or its tendency.
- Limiting friction: F_lim = μ_s N
- Kinetic friction (simple model): F_k = μ_k N
μ_s and μ_k are coefficients of friction; N is the normal reaction.
Usually μ_k is less than μ_s for the same pair of surfaces, but real surfaces can vary.

3.6 Equilibrium and torque
- Translational equilibrium: net force = 0.
- Rotational equilibrium: net torque = 0.
- Torque magnitude: τ = rF sinθ.
- For perpendicular force: τ = F × perpendicular distance.
Unit: N m.
Principle of moments: in rotational equilibrium, total clockwise moment equals total anticlockwise moment about the same point.

3.7 Hooke’s law (within elastic limit)
Stress ∝ strain.
Young’s modulus = longitudinal stress / longitudinal strain.
Bulk modulus = pressure change / volumetric strain (magnitude convention).
Shear modulus = shear stress / shear strain.
Spring form: F = −kx; the negative sign shows restoring force direction.
Spring potential energy: U = ½kx².

============================================================
4. WORK, ENERGY AND POWER
============================================================

4.1 Work
For constant force:
W = Fs cosθ
If force and displacement are in the same direction: W = Fs.
If perpendicular: W = 0.
If opposite: work done by the force is negative.
Unit: joule (J).

4.2 Kinetic and potential energy
- Kinetic energy: KE = ½mv²
- Gravitational potential energy near Earth: PE = mgh
- Elastic potential energy in a spring: PE = ½kx²
- Mechanical energy = KE + PE (when those are the relevant forms).
Law of conservation of energy: energy is transferred or transformed; total energy of an isolated system is conserved.

4.3 Power
- Average power: P = W/t
- For a force parallel to velocity: P = Fv
- General instantaneous mechanical power: P = F·v
Unit: watt (W).
1 kW = 1000 W.
1 horsepower is approximately 746 W (mechanical horsepower).

4.4 Efficiency
Efficiency = (useful output energy or power / total input energy or power) × 100%.
Efficiency cannot exceed 100% for an ordinary energy-conversion device when all inputs and outputs are counted correctly.

============================================================
5. GRAVITATION AND CIRCULAR MOTION
============================================================

5.1 Newton’s universal law of gravitation
F = Gm₁m₂/r²
G = universal gravitational constant ≈ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
r is the distance between the centres of the two masses.

5.2 Acceleration due to gravity
- Weight: W = mg
- Near Earth’s surface: g ≈ 9.8 m/s² (often approximated as 10 m/s² in simple problems).
- At distance r from Earth’s centre: g = GM/r².
- At height h above Earth: g_h = GM/(R+h)².
- At depth d below the surface, the simplified uniform-density model gives g_d ≈ g(1 − d/R). This is an idealised model.

5.3 Mass and weight
- Mass measures inertia and is measured in kg; it is approximately constant with location.
- Weight is gravitational force, measured in newtons; W = mg and varies with g.

5.4 Satellites and orbital motion
For a circular orbit:
- Orbital speed: v = √(GM/r)
- Orbital period: T = 2π√(r³/GM)
- Escape speed from a spherical body (ignoring atmosphere and rotation): v_e = √(2GM/R) = √(2gR) near the surface.
- Gravitational potential energy for two masses: U = −GMm/r, taking zero at infinity.
- Gravitational potential per unit mass: φ = −GM/r.
For an ideal circular satellite orbit, total mechanical energy = −GMm/(2r).

6. PRESSURE, DENSITY AND FLUID LAWS
============================================================

6.1 Density and relative density
- Density: ρ = m/V.
- Relative density = density of substance / density of reference water (for solids/liquids, commonly at a specified reference temperature); it is dimensionless.

6.2 Pressure
- Pressure: P = F/A for force normal to a surface.
- Liquid pressure at depth h: P_gauge = ρgh.
- Absolute pressure = atmospheric pressure + gauge pressure.
- Pressure in a static fluid increases with depth and density.

6.3 Pascal’s law
A pressure change applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished throughout the fluid and to the container walls.
Hydraulic press/lift ideal relation:
F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂
Thus F₂ = F₁(A₂/A₁), neglecting losses.

6.4 Archimedes’ principle and buoyancy
An immersed body experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced:
F_b = ρ_fluid V_displaced g.
- A body floats when buoyancy balances its weight.
- Fraction submerged for a floating uniform object: V_submerged/V_total = ρ_object/ρ_fluid.

6.5 Continuity equation and Bernoulli’s principle
For steady incompressible flow:
A₁v₁ = A₂v₂.
For ideal steady flow of an incompressible, non-viscous fluid along a streamline:
P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant.
This is Bernoulli’s equation; practical applications require attention to assumptions and energy losses.

6.6 Stokes’ law
For a small sphere moving slowly through a viscous fluid under laminar-flow conditions:
F_d = 6πηrv
η = dynamic viscosity, r = sphere radius, v = relative speed.
Terminal speed depends on gravity, buoyancy, size, and viscosity.

7. ELASTICITY, SURFACE TENSION AND VISCOSITY
============================================================

7.1 Stress and strain
- Stress = force / area; unit Pa.
- Longitudinal strain = change in length / original length; dimensionless.
- Young’s modulus Y = longitudinal stress / longitudinal strain.
- Elastic limit: greatest deformation range over which a body returns to its original form after the deforming force is removed (ideal description).

7.2 Surface tension
Surface tension is force per unit length along a liquid surface:
γ = F/L (for a single line of contact under the usual definition).
Unit: N/m.
Surface energy per unit area is numerically equal to surface tension for a simple liquid interface.
Small liquid drops tend to be spherical because this reduces surface area for a given volume.

7.3 Capillary rise
For a circular capillary tube:
h = 2γ cosθ/(ρgr)
γ = surface tension; θ = contact angle; ρ = liquid density; r = tube radius.
For water wetting clean glass, cosθ is close to 1. Capillary rise is greater in narrower tubes under the same conditions.

7.4 Viscosity
Dynamic viscosity η measures resistance to relative flow between fluid layers.
For Newtonian fluids, shear stress is proportional to velocity gradient.
Kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ; unit m²/s.

8. HEAT AND THERMAL PHYSICS
============================================================

8.1 Temperature conversions
- Kelvin: T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15.
- Celsius: t(°C) = T(K) − 273.15.
- Fahrenheit: F = (9/5)C + 32.
- Celsius: C = (5/9)(F − 32).
Absolute zero = 0 K = −273.15 °C.

8.2 Heat and specific heat capacity
- Heat gained/lost without phase change: Q = mcΔT.
- Specific heat capacity: c = Q/(mΔT).
- Unit of c: J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
- Heat capacity: C = Q/ΔT = mc for a uniform sample.

8.3 Latent heat
During a phase change at constant pressure and transition temperature:
Q = mL
L = specific latent heat; unit J/kg.
- Latent heat of fusion: solid ↔ liquid.
- Latent heat of vaporisation: liquid ↔ gas.

8.4 Thermal expansion
- Linear expansion: ΔL = αL₀ΔT.
- Area expansion (isotropic solid, small temperature change): ΔA ≈ 2αA₀ΔT.
- Volume expansion (isotropic solid, small temperature change): ΔV ≈ 3αV₀ΔT.
α = coefficient of linear expansion.
The approximations for area and volume assume small expansion and isotropic material.

8.5 Heat transfer
- Conduction: transfer through matter without bulk movement.
- Convection: transfer by bulk motion in fluids.
- Radiation: transfer by electromagnetic waves; no material medium is required.
Fourier’s law in one-dimensional form: heat-transfer rate is proportional to thermal conductivity, area and temperature gradient; for a uniform slab, rate ≈ kA(ΔT/L).

8.6 Gas laws
For a fixed amount of ideal gas:
- Boyle’s law (constant T): P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
- Charles’s law (constant P): V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂.
- Pressure law (constant V): P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂.
- Combined gas law: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂.
Temperatures must be in kelvin.
- Ideal gas equation: PV = nRT.
R = universal gas constant ≈ 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

8.7 Thermodynamics
- First law: ΔU = Q − W, where W is work done by the system.
- For a cyclic process, change in internal energy over a full cycle is zero.
- At constant volume, expansion work is zero for a simple P–V process.
- Heat naturally flows from higher to lower temperature; a refrigerator needs external work to transfer heat from a colder region to a warmer region.

9. WAVES AND SOUND
============================================================

9.1 Wave equation
v = fλ
v = wave speed; f = frequency; λ = wavelength.
- Period: T = 1/f.
- Angular frequency: ω = 2πf.
- Wave number: k = 2π/λ.
- For a travelling sinusoidal wave: y = A sin(kx − ωt + φ) is one common form.

9.2 Sound
Sound is a mechanical wave and requires a medium. In air under ordinary conditions it is longitudinal.
- Approximate speed in air near 20°C: 343 m/s (varies with temperature and conditions).
- Echo distance: d = vt/2, where t is the total time for the sound to travel to the reflecting surface and back.
- Minimum time gap for a distinct echo is commonly taken as about 0.1 s in basic school problems; actual perception depends on conditions.
- Ultrasound: frequency greater than 20 kHz.
- Infrasound: frequency less than 20 Hz.
- Audible range for a typical young human: approximately 20 Hz–20 kHz; individual hearing varies.

9.3 String and air-column relations
For a stretched string fixed at both ends:
- Fundamental frequency: f₁ = v/(2L).
- Harmonics: f_n = nv/(2L), n = 1,2,3…
For an ideal pipe open at both ends:
- Fundamental: f₁ = v/(2L).
For an ideal pipe closed at one end:
- Fundamental: f₁ = v/(4L); odd harmonics only in the ideal model.
End correction may be needed in real pipes.

9.4 Intensity and decibels
- Intensity I = power / area.
- For a point source spreading uniformly in free space, I ∝ 1/r².
- Sound level: β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) dB, with reference intensity I₀ conventionally 10⁻¹² W/m² in air.
Decibels are logarithmic; a rise of 10 dB corresponds to ten times the intensity.

10. LIGHT, MIRRORS AND LENSES
============================================================

10.1 Reflection
- Angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
- Incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane.
- Plane mirror: image is virtual, erect, same size, laterally inverted; image distance equals object distance in magnitude.

10.2 Spherical mirrors
Using the common Cartesian sign convention:
- Mirror formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u.
- Magnification: m = h_i/h_o = −v/u.
- Radius of curvature: R = 2f (for a spherical mirror, paraxial approximation).
The sign of u, v and f must follow one consistent convention.

10.3 Refraction
- Snell’s law: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r.
- Refractive index: n = c/v (for a medium, relative to vacuum).
- For air-to-medium approximately: n ≈ sin i/sin r.
- Total internal reflection requires light to travel from optically denser to optically rarer medium and incidence angle greater than the critical angle.
- Critical angle for denser medium to air: sin C = 1/n, when air’s refractive index is approximated as 1.

10.4 Thin lenses
Using the common Cartesian sign convention:
- Lens formula: 1/f = 1/v − 1/u.
- Magnification: m = h_i/h_o = v/u.
- Power: P = 1/f (f in metres), unit dioptre.
- Lenses in contact: P_total = P₁ + P₂ + …
A converging (convex) lens has positive focal length in the usual convention; a diverging (concave) lens has negative focal length.

10.5 Lens maker’s formula
For a thin lens in air:
1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), using the usual sign convention for radii of curvature.

10.6 Dispersion and optical effects
- Dispersion: splitting of white light into component colours because refractive index depends on wavelength.
- In a glass prism under ordinary dispersion, violet light deviates more than red.
- Scattering intensity for very small particles in the Rayleigh regime varies approximately as 1/λ⁴; shorter wavelengths scatter more strongly.
- A rainbow involves refraction, dispersion and internal reflection in water droplets.

11. ELECTRICITY AND ELECTRIC CIRCUITS
============================================================

11.1 Charge and current
- Charge: Q = It for constant current.
- Current: I = Q/t.
- Conventional current direction is the direction positive charge would move; in metal wires electron drift is opposite.
- Charge is measured in coulombs (C); current in amperes (A).

11.2 Potential difference and Ohm’s law
- Potential difference: V = W/Q.
- Ohm’s law: V = IR, for a conductor under constant physical conditions.
- Resistance: R = V/I.
- Resistivity: ρ = RA/L.
- Conductance: G = 1/R.
- Conductivity: σ = 1/ρ.
Unit of resistivity: Ω m; conductivity: S/m.

11.3 Series and parallel resistors
Series:
- R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + …
- Same current passes through each resistor.
- Supply voltage is divided among components.
Parallel:
- 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …
- Same potential difference across each branch.
- Total current is the sum of branch currents.
For two resistors in parallel: R_eq = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂).

11.4 Electrical power and energy
- P = VI
- P = I²R
- P = V²/R
- Electrical energy: E = Pt = VIt.
- Heating effect: H = I²Rt for a resistor with constant resistance.
- 1 kilowatt-hour (kWh) = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
A kWh is a unit of energy, not power.

11.5 Cells and internal resistance
For a cell with emf ε and internal resistance r delivering current I:
- Terminal voltage V = ε − Ir (discharging).
- Simple circuit current: I = ε/(R + r).
For cells in series, emfs add algebraically; for identical cells in parallel, ideal equivalent emf stays the same while effective internal resistance decreases.

11.6 Kirchhoff’s laws
- Junction rule: total current entering a junction equals total current leaving it (charge conservation).
- Loop rule: algebraic sum of potential changes around a closed loop is zero (energy conservation).
Choose current directions and voltage signs consistently.

12. MAGNETISM AND ELECTROMAGNETIC INDUCTION
============================================================

12.1 Magnetic force
- Force on a straight current-carrying conductor: F = BIL sinθ.
- Magnetic force on a moving charge: F = qvB sinθ.
- Circular path radius for a charged particle moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field: r = mv/(|q|B).
- Magnetic flux: Φ = BA cosθ for a uniform field through a flat area, where θ is between B and the area normal.
SI unit of magnetic flux: weber (Wb).

12.2 Electromagnetic induction
Faraday’s law:
- Induced emf ε = −N(dΦ/dt), for N turns.
The minus sign represents Lenz’s law: the induced effect opposes the change in magnetic flux that produces it.
- Motional emf for a rod of length L moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field: ε = BLv.
- Transformer ideal ratio: V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p.
- For an ideal transformer: V_p I_p ≈ V_s I_s.
A transformer changes AC voltage; it does not operate by steady DC in the normal way because changing flux is required.

12.3 Electric motor and generator
- Motor: electrical energy → mechanical energy.
- Generator: mechanical energy → electrical energy.
- Electromagnetic induction is the operating principle of a generator.
- Fleming’s left-hand rule: direction of force/motion in a motor.
- Fleming’s right-hand rule: direction of induced current in a generator.
- Right-hand thumb rule: magnetic field direction around a straight current-carrying conductor.

12.4 Alternating current
For sinusoidal AC:
- V_rms = V₀/√2.
- I_rms = I₀/√2.
- Average power for a resistive load: P = V_rms I_rms.
For a general AC circuit: P_avg = V_rms I_rms cosφ, where cosφ is the power factor.

13. ELECTRONICS AND COMMUNICATION
============================================================

13.1 Basic semiconductor ideas
- Semiconductor conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator and changes with temperature, doping and illumination.
- n-type semiconductor: donor doping provides electrons as majority carriers.
- p-type semiconductor: acceptor doping produces holes as majority carriers.
- A p–n junction diode conducts strongly in forward bias and offers high resistance in reverse bias under ordinary conditions (ignoring breakdown and leakage).
- Rectifier: converts AC into pulsating DC.
- Transistor: can act as an amplifier or switch.
- LED: emits light when forward biased.
- Photodiode: converts incident light into an electrical signal.
- Solar cell: converts light energy into electrical energy through the photovoltaic effect.

13.2 Communication
- Information can be transmitted using electromagnetic waves.
- Modulation changes a carrier-wave property according to the information signal.
- AM: amplitude of carrier varies.
- FM: frequency of carrier varies.
- Digital communication represents information using discrete symbols/bits.
- Optical fibre guides light mainly by total internal reflection.
- Laser light is highly directional, coherent and often nearly monochromatic compared with ordinary light.

14. ATOMIC AND NUCLEAR PHYSICS
============================================================

14.1 Photon and quantum relations
- Photon energy: E = hf = hc/λ.
- Planck constant: h ≈ 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
- Speed of light in vacuum: c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.
- Photoelectric equation: K_max = hf − φ, when photon energy exceeds work function φ.
- de Broglie wavelength: λ = h/p for a particle with momentum p.

14.2 Radioactivity
- Radioactive decay law: N = N₀e^(−λt).
- Activity: A = λN.
- Half-life: T₁/₂ = ln 2/λ ≈ 0.693/λ.
- After n half-lives: N = N₀(1/2)^n.
Radioactive decay is spontaneous and, for ordinary conditions, is not controlled by temperature or pressure in the way chemical reaction rates are.

14.3 Nuclear energy
- Mass–energy relation: E = mc².
- Mass defect: Δm = (sum of separated nucleon masses) − (actual nuclear mass), with care about electron masses in the chosen convention.
- Binding energy: BE = Δm c².
- Nuclear fission: a heavy nucleus splits into lighter nuclei, releasing energy in suitable reactions.
- Nuclear fusion: light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy in suitable reactions.
- Alpha radiation: helium nucleus; charge +2e; relatively high ionising power and low penetration.
- Beta-minus radiation: electron; charge −e; moderate penetration compared with alpha.
- Gamma radiation: high-energy electromagnetic radiation; no electric charge; high penetration relative to alpha and beta.
Radiation safety depends on type, energy, exposure time, distance and shielding.

COMMON EXAM TRAPS
1. Distance is scalar; displacement is vector.
2. Speed is scalar; velocity is vector.
3. Zero displacement does not necessarily mean zero distance.
4. Action and reaction act on different bodies.
5. Mass is measured in kg; weight is a force measured in N.
6. Work can be zero even when a force acts, if displacement is perpendicular to that force.
7. kWh is a unit of energy; kW is a unit of power.
8. Heat and temperature are different physical quantities.
9. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum; electromagnetic waves can.
10. Frequency is measured in hertz; wavelength in metres.
11. A convex lens usually converges parallel rays; a concave lens diverges them.
12. Ohm’s law applies when physical conditions such as temperature remain constant.
13. In a series circuit, current is common; in parallel, voltage is common.
14. A transformer requires changing magnetic flux and normally works with AC.
15. The minus sign in Faraday’s law represents Lenz’s law.
16. Alpha particles are nuclei; gamma rays are electromagnetic waves.
17. Always convert centimetres to metres and Celsius to kelvin where required.
18. Lens/mirror formula signs depend on the sign convention; do not mix conventions.
19. Use SI units consistently.
20. Dimensional consistency is necessary but not sufficient to prove a formula.

18. PRACTICE MCQs (10)

1. A body starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 2 m/s² for 5 s. Its final velocity is:
A) 5 m/s  B) 10 m/s  C) 20 m/s  D) 25 m/s
Answer: B. v = u + at = 0 + 2 × 5 = 10 m/s.

2. Which quantity is equal to the area under a velocity–time graph?
A) Acceleration  B) Force  C) Displacement  D) Momentum
Answer: C. The signed area under the velocity–time graph gives displacement.

3. A force of 20 N moves a body 3 m in the direction of the force. Work done is:
A) 6 J  B) 17 J  C) 23 J  D) 60 J
Answer: D. W = Fs = 20 × 3 = 60 J.

4. The pressure at a point in a static liquid depends directly on:
A) Colour of liquid  B) Depth and density  C) Shape of vessel only  D) Surface area only
Answer: B. Gauge pressure is ρgh.

5. A 100 W device operates for 2 hours. Electrical energy used is:
A) 0.02 kWh  B) 0.2 kWh  C) 2 kWh  D) 200 kWh
Answer: B. 100 W = 0.1 kW; energy = 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 kWh.

6. If a wave has frequency 200 Hz and wavelength 1.5 m, its speed is:
A) 75 m/s  B) 133.3 m/s  C) 201.5 m/s  D) 300 m/s
Answer: D. v = fλ = 200 × 1.5 = 300 m/s.

7. A lens has focal length +0.5 m. Its power is:
A) −2 D  B) +0.5 D  C) +2 D  D) +5 D
Answer: C. P = 1/f = 1/0.5 = +2 D.

8. Two resistors of 6 Ω and 3 Ω are connected in parallel. Equivalent resistance is:
A) 9 Ω  B) 3 Ω  C) 2 Ω  D) 18 Ω
Answer: C. R = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 18/9 = 2 Ω.

9. Which law describes the direction of induced current opposing the change in magnetic flux?
A) Ohm’s law  B) Lenz’s law  C) Pascal’s law  D) Hooke’s law
Answer: B. Lenz’s law expresses the opposition represented by the negative sign in Faraday’s law.

10. The half-life of a radioactive sample is 4 days. The fraction remaining after 12 days is:
A) 1/2  B) 1/4  C) 1/8  D) 1/16
Answer: C. Twelve days is three half-lives, so remaining fraction = (1/2)³ = 1/8.
