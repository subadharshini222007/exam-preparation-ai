## 1.1 Definition of Biology

Biology is the branch of science that deals with the study of living organisms, their structure, functions, growth, origin, evolution, distribution and interactions with the environment.

The word Biology is derived from two Greek words:

* Bios — Life
* Logos — Study

Biology helps us understand humans, animals, plants, microorganisms and their relationships with the environment.

Biology is important in:

1. Medicine and healthcare.
2. Agriculture and crop improvement.
3. Environmental conservation.
4. Biotechnology.
5. Food production.
6. Disease prevention.
7. Genetic engineering.
8. Wildlife conservation.

# CHAPTER 2: CELL BIOLOGY

## 2.1 What Is a Cell?

A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

All living organisms are made of one or more cells.

Examples:

* Amoeba consists of a single cell.
* Bacteria are unicellular organisms.
* Humans are multicellular organisms.
* Most plants are multicellular organisms.

Cells perform essential functions such as:

* Respiration.
* Nutrition.
* Growth.
* Excretion.
* Reproduction.
* Protein synthesis.
* Response to stimuli.

## 2.2 Cell Theory

The classical cell theory was developed through the work of Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann.

The major principles are:

1. All living organisms are composed of cells.
2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
3. New cells arise from pre-existing cells, a principle associated with Rudolf Virchow.

### Importance of Cell Theory

Cell theory provides a common foundation for understanding the structure and function of living organisms.

It explains why cells are essential for:

* Growth.
* Repair.
* Reproduction.
* Maintenance of tissues and organs.

### TNPSC Questions

**Question 1:** Who coined the term cell?

Answer: Robert Hooke.

**Question 2:** Who proposed that new cells arise from pre-existing cells?

Answer: Rudolf Virchow is associated with this principle.

**Question 3:** What is the basic structural and functional unit of life?

Answer: Cell.

---

## 2.3 Types of Cells

Cells are broadly classified into two major types:

1. Prokaryotic cells.
2. Eukaryotic cells.

### Prokaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic cells do not have a membrane-bound nucleus.

Their genetic material is located in a region called the nucleoid.

Examples:

* Bacteria.
* Archaea.

Characteristics:

* Usually smaller and simpler than eukaryotic cells.
* Lack membrane-bound organelles.
* Possess ribosomes.
* Have DNA.
* Reproduce commonly through binary fission.

### Eukaryotic Cells

Eukaryotic cells contain a membrane-bound nucleus.

They also contain membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria and, in plants, chloroplasts.

Examples:

* Animal cells.
* Plant cells.
* Fungal cells.
* Protist cells.

Characteristics:

* Have a nucleus.
* Contain specialised organelles.
* Have organised internal compartments.
* Can form unicellular or multicellular organisms.

### Difference Between Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

| Feature                   | Prokaryotic Cell                        | Eukaryotic Cell                              |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| True nucleus              | Absent                                  | Present                                      |
| Nuclear membrane          | Absent                                  | Present                                      |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent                                  | Present in typical examples                  |
| DNA                       | Usually circular in the main chromosome | Nuclear chromosomes are generally linear     |
| Ribosomes                 | 70S-type                                | Cytoplasmic ribosomes are generally 80S-type |
| Cell size                 | Usually smaller                         | Usually larger                               |
| Examples                  | Bacteria and Archaea                    | Plants, animals and fungi                    |

**Important note:** Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own ribosomes, which differ from the cytoplasmic ribosomes of eukaryotic cells.

# CHAPTER 3: CELL ORGANELLES

Cell organelles are specialised structures inside cells that perform particular functions.

## 3.1 Cell Membrane

The cell membrane is a selectively permeable boundary surrounding the cell.

It controls the movement of substances into and out of the cell.

### Structure

The membrane mainly consists of a phospholipid bilayer containing proteins.

Some membranes also contain cholesterol and carbohydrate-containing molecules.

### Functions

1. Maintains the boundary of the cell.
2. Controls the transport of substances.
3. Helps cells communicate with their surroundings.
4. Participates in cell recognition.
5. Maintains internal conditions.

### Transport Across the Cell Membrane

**Diffusion:** Movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration.

**Osmosis:** Movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane from higher water potential to lower water potential.

**Active transport:** Movement of substances against their concentration or electrochemical gradient using energy.

**Facilitated diffusion:** Movement of substances through membrane proteins without direct energy expenditure.

### TNPSC Example

When a raisin is placed in water, water enters the raisin by osmosis, causing it to swell.

---

## 3.2 Nucleus

The nucleus is a membrane-bound organelle containing most of the genetic material in a eukaryotic cell.

### Structure

The nucleus contains:

* Nuclear envelope.
* Nuclear pores.
* Nucleoplasm.
* Chromatin.
* Nucleolus.

### Functions

1. Stores genetic information.
2. Helps regulate gene expression.
3. Participates in cell growth and division.
4. Controls many cellular activities through gene regulation.

### Nucleolus

The nucleolus is a region inside the nucleus involved in ribosomal RNA production and ribosome assembly.

**TNPSC point:** The nucleus is often called the control centre of the eukaryotic cell because it contains genetic information that directs many cellular activities.

---

## 3.3 Mitochondria

Mitochondria are organelles involved in aerobic cellular respiration and ATP production.

ATP stands for Adenosine Triphosphate.

ATP acts as an energy-transfer molecule in cells.

### Structure

Mitochondria have:

* An outer membrane.
* An inner membrane.
* Intermembrane space.
* Matrix.
* Cristae, which are folds of the inner membrane.

### Functions

1. Participate in aerobic respiration.
2. Produce much of the ATP in many eukaryotic cells.
3. Help regulate aspects of cellular metabolism.
4. Participate in certain cell-signalling processes.

Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes.

**TNPSC Important Point:** Mitochondria are commonly described as the powerhouse of the cell.

### Cellular Respiration

A simplified overall equation for aerobic glucose respiration is:

$$
C_6H_{12}O_6+6O_2
\rightarrow 6CO_2+6H_2O+\text{energy}
$$

The released energy is captured partly in ATP.

**Important:** This is an overall summary equation, not a single-step reaction.

---

## 3.4 Ribosomes

Ribosomes are cellular structures responsible for protein synthesis.

They translate information carried by messenger RNA into a chain of amino acids.

### Functions

1. Read messenger RNA.
2. Join amino acids in the correct sequence.
3. Produce proteins.

Ribosomes may be found:

* Free in the cytoplasm.
* Attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum.

### TNPSC Point

**Question:** Which cell structure is responsible for protein synthesis?

Answer: Ribosomes.

---

## 3.5 Endoplasmic Reticulum

The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membranes within eukaryotic cells.

It occurs in two main forms.

### Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum

Rough endoplasmic reticulum has ribosomes attached to its surface.

Functions:

* Synthesis of many secreted proteins.
* Production of membrane proteins.
* Initial processing and folding of certain proteins.

### Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum

Smooth endoplasmic reticulum lacks attached ribosomes.

Functions:

* Lipid synthesis.
* Detoxification of certain substances.
* Calcium storage in specialised cells.

### Difference

| Rough ER                                      | Smooth ER                                               |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ribosomes attached                            | Ribosomes absent                                        |
| Important in protein synthesis and processing | Important in lipid synthesis and other functions        |
| Often prominent in protein-secreting cells    | Often prominent in lipid-producing or detoxifying cells |

---

## 3.6 Golgi Apparatus

The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts and packages many proteins and lipids.

### Functions

1. Modifies certain proteins and lipids.
2. Sorts cellular products.
3. Packages materials into vesicles.
4. Helps direct substances to their destinations.
5. Participates in the formation of certain lysosomes.

**TNPSC memory point:** Golgi apparatus — packaging and distribution centre of the cell.

---

## 3.7 Lysosomes

Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles containing digestive enzymes.

### Functions

1. Break down certain macromolecules.
2. Digest materials taken into the cell.
3. Help recycle damaged cellular components.
4. Participate in cellular waste processing.

They are especially associated with intracellular digestion.

**TNPSC point:** Lysosomes are often called the digestive bags of the cell.

---

## 3.8 Chloroplasts

Chloroplasts are photosynthetic organelles found in plants and many algae.

They contain chlorophyll, the pigment that absorbs light energy.

### Structure

Important components include:

* Outer membrane.
* Inner membrane.
* Stroma.
* Thylakoids.
* Grana.

### Functions

1. Capture light energy.
2. Carry out photosynthesis.
3. Help produce carbohydrates from carbon dioxide and water using light energy.

### Photosynthesis Equation

A simplified overall equation is:

$$
6CO_2+6H_2O
\xrightarrow{\text{light}}
C_6H_{12}O_6+6O_2
$$

This equation summarises the process; the oxygen released during photosynthesis comes from water.

**TNPSC Point:** Chloroplasts are the principal sites of photosynthesis in plant cells.

---

## 3.9 Vacuoles

Vacuoles are membrane-bound compartments used for storage and other functions.

In many mature plant cells, a large central vacuole occupies much of the cell volume.

### Functions

1. Stores water.
2. Stores dissolved substances.
3. Helps maintain turgor pressure.
4. Participates in waste storage and cellular regulation.

### Plant Cell vs Animal Cell

Plant cells often have a large central vacuole, whereas animal cells generally have smaller vesicles and vacuoles.

---

## 3.10 Cell Wall

The cell wall is a relatively rigid external layer present in plants, fungi and many other organisms.

Plant cell walls are mainly composed of cellulose.

Fungal cell walls contain chitin and other substances.

Bacterial cell walls typically contain peptidoglycan.

### Functions

1. Provides structural support.
2. Helps maintain cell shape.
3. Protects the cell.
4. Helps prevent excessive expansion under suitable conditions.

**TNPSC point:** Cellulose is a major component of plant cell walls.

# CHAPTER 4: PLANT CELLS AND ANIMAL CELLS

## 4.1 Plant Cells

Plant cells typically contain:

* Cell wall.
* Cell membrane.
* Nucleus.
* Cytoplasm.
* Mitochondria.
* Chloroplasts in photosynthetic cells.

# CHAPTER 5: CELL DIVISION

Cell division is the process by which a parent cell produces daughter cells.

Two major types studied at this level are:

1. Mitosis.
2. Meiosis.

## 5.1 Mitosis

Mitosis is a type of nuclear division that usually results in two daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent cell.

### Stages of Mitosis

**1. Prophase**

Chromosomes condense and become visible. The mitotic spindle begins to form.

**2. Metaphase**

Chromosomes align at the cell's equatorial region.

**3. Anaphase**

Sister chromatids separate and move towards opposite poles.

**4. Telophase**

New nuclei form around the separated chromosome sets.

Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm and commonly follows or overlaps with the later stages of nuclear division.

### Importance of Mitosis

1. Growth.
2. Tissue repair.
3. Replacement of certain damaged or old cells.
4. Asexual reproduction in some organisms.

**TNPSC point:** Mitosis generally maintains chromosome number.

## 5.2 Meiosis

Meiosis is a specialised type of cell division that reduces the chromosome number by half.

It is involved in the formation of haploid reproductive cells or their precursors.

### Important Features

1. One round of DNA replication occurs before meiosis.
2. Two successive divisions take place.
3. Homologous chromosomes separate during meiosis I.
4. Sister chromatids separate during meiosis II.
5. Genetic variation can arise through crossing over and independent assortment.

### Difference Between Mitosis and Meiosis

| Feature                | Mitosis                         | Meiosis                                          |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Number of divisions    | One                             | Two                                              |
| Typical daughter cells | Two                             | Four haploid cells in a typical complete example |
| Chromosome number      | Usually maintained              | Halved                                           |
| Crossing over          | Not a normal feature of mitosis | Occurs during prophase I                         |
| Main role              | Growth and repair               | Sexual reproduction and genetic variation        |

### Example: Human Chromosome Number

Most human somatic cells contain 46 chromosomes.

Human gametes typically contain 23 chromosomes.

During fertilisation:

$$
23+23=46
$$

The fusion of a sperm and an egg restores the diploid chromosome number.

# CHAPTER 6: TISSUES

A tissue is a group of cells that work together to perform particular functions.

## 6.1 Plant Tissues

Plant tissues can be broadly classified into:

1. Meristematic tissues.
2. Permanent tissues.

### Meristematic Tissues

Meristematic tissues contain actively dividing cells.

They are responsible for plant growth.

Types include:

**Apical meristem:** Present near root and shoot tips; contributes to primary growth.

**Lateral meristem:** Contributes to an increase in thickness or girth.

**Intercalary meristem:** Found at certain regions such as the bases of leaves or internodes in some plants.

### Permanent Tissues

Permanent tissues consist of cells that have differentiated to perform specific functions.

#### Simple Permanent Tissues

**Parenchyma**

* Usually living cells.
* Storage and other functions.
* May perform photosynthesis when chloroplasts are present.

**Collenchyma**

* Living cells with unevenly thickened walls.
* Provides flexible support in growing plant parts.

**Sclerenchyma**

* Usually dead at maturity.
* Thick, often lignified walls.
* Provides mechanical strength.

#### Complex Permanent Tissues

**Xylem**

* Transports water and dissolved minerals from roots to other plant parts.
* Also contributes to structural support.

**Phloem**

* Transports sugars and other organic substances through the plant.

**TNPSC memory point:**

* Xylem — water and mineral transport.
* Phloem — transport of sugars and other organic materials.

## 6.2 Animal Tissues

The four major types of animal tissues are:

1. Epithelial tissue.
2. Connective tissue.
3. Muscular tissue.
4. Nervous tissue.

### Epithelial Tissue

Epithelial tissue covers body surfaces and lines many internal cavities and organs.

Functions:

* Protection.
* Absorption.
* Secretion.
* Filtration in specialised locations.

Examples:

* Skin epidermis.
* Intestinal lining.
* Certain glandular tissues.

### Connective Tissue

Connective tissue supports, binds and connects different structures.

Examples:

* Bone.
* Cartilage.
* Blood.
* Adipose tissue.
* Tendons.
* Ligaments.

**Tendon:** Connects muscle to bone.

**Ligament:** Connects bone to bone.

### Muscular Tissue

Muscular tissue contracts to produce movement.

Three major types:

**Skeletal muscle**

* Usually under voluntary control.
* Helps body movement.

**Smooth muscle**

* Involuntary.
* Found in organs such as the intestines and blood vessels.

**Cardiac muscle**

* Forms the muscular wall of the heart.
* Contracts rhythmically.

### Nervous Tissue

Nervous tissue receives and transmits signals.

It consists mainly of neurons and supporting glial cells.

Functions:

* Communication.
* Coordination.
* Sensory processing.
* Control of body activities.

# CHAPTER 7: IMPORTANT BIOLOGY FORMULAS

Biology contains fewer numerical formulas than Physics or Chemistry, but some equations and calculations are important for competitive exams.

## 7.1 Magnification

Magnification describes how many times larger an image appears than the actual object.

$$
\boxed{\text{Magnification}=
\frac{\text{Image size}}{\text{Actual size}}}
$$

Both sizes must use the same units.

### Solved Sum

A cell is 20 μm long. Its image is 100 μm long. Calculate magnification.

$$
\text{Magnification}=\frac{100}{20}
$$

$$
\boxed{5\times}
$$

Answer: 5 times.

## 7.2 Photosynthesis

Simplified equation:

$$
6CO_2+6H_2O
\xrightarrow{\text{light}}
C_6H_{12}O_6+6O_2
$$

Important requirements:

* Light energy.
* Chlorophyll in photosynthetic cells.
* Carbon dioxide.
* Water.

## 7.3 Aerobic Respiration

Simplified equation:

$$
C_6H_{12}O_6+6O_2
\rightarrow6CO_2+6H_2O+\text{energy}
$$

The equation summarises aerobic respiration of glucose.

## 7.4 Population Growth Rate

A simple population growth-rate formula is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Growth rate}=
\frac{\text{Final population}-\text{Initial population}}
{\text{Initial population}}\times100}
$$

### Solved Sum

An organism's population increases from 200 to 250.

$$
\text{Growth rate}=\frac{250-200}{200}\times100
$$

$$
=\frac{50}{200}\times100
$$

$$
\boxed{25\%}
$$

Answer: 25% increase.

# PART 2 — HUMAN DIGESTIVE SYSTEM, NUTRITION AND RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

## 1. HUMAN DIGESTIVE SYSTEM

### 1.1 Introduction

The human digestive system is responsible for breaking down complex food substances into simpler, soluble substances that can be absorbed and utilized by the body.

The major functions of the digestive system are:

1. Ingestion of food.
2. Mechanical digestion of food.
3. Chemical digestion of food.
4. Absorption of nutrients.
5. Assimilation of absorbed nutrients.
6. Elimination of undigested waste.

The human digestive system consists of the alimentary canal and associated digestive glands.

### 1.2 Parts of the Alimentary Canal

The alimentary canal is a long muscular tube extending from the mouth to the anus.

Its main parts are:

1. Mouth or buccal cavity.
2. Pharynx.
3. Oesophagus.
4. Stomach.
5. Small intestine.
6. Large intestine.
7. Rectum.
8. Anus.

### 1.3 Mouth and Buccal Cavity

Digestion begins in the mouth.

The teeth break food into smaller pieces through mechanical digestion. The tongue mixes food with saliva and helps in swallowing.

**Functions of saliva:**

* Moistens and lubricates food.
* Helps form a soft mass called a bolus.
* Contains salivary amylase.
* Begins the digestion of starch into simpler carbohydrates, including maltose and shorter carbohydrate chains.

Saliva is secreted by three major pairs of salivary glands:

* Parotid glands.
* Submandibular glands.
* Sublingual glands.

**TNPSC Important Facts**

* Digestion begins in the mouth.
* Salivary amylase acts on starch.
* The tongue helps in tasting, mixing and swallowing food.
* Teeth perform mechanical digestion.

### 1.4 Pharynx and Oesophagus

The pharynx acts as a common passage for food and air.

The oesophagus carries food from the pharynx to the stomach through rhythmic muscular contractions called **peristalsis**.

Peristalsis is the wave-like contraction and relaxation of the muscles of the alimentary canal.

**Important:** Food moves through the oesophagus mainly by peristalsis, not simply by gravity.

### 1.5 Stomach

The stomach is a muscular, J-shaped organ located in the upper abdomen.

It temporarily stores food, mixes food with gastric juice and begins the major chemical digestion of proteins.

The stomach contains gastric glands that secrete:

* Hydrochloric acid (HCl).
* Pepsinogen.
* Mucus.
* Intrinsic factor.

**Functions of hydrochloric acid:**

1. Creates an acidic environment.
2. Helps activate pepsinogen into pepsin.
3. Helps destroy many microorganisms entering with food.

**Functions of pepsin:**

Pepsin is a protein-digesting enzyme. It breaks proteins into smaller peptides.

**Functions of mucus:**

Mucus protects the stomach lining from acid and digestive enzymes.

**Functions of intrinsic factor:**

Intrinsic factor is required for the normal absorption of vitamin B12 in the terminal ileum of the small intestine.

**TNPSC Important Facts**

* Pepsin digests proteins.
* Hydrochloric acid provides the acidic medium in the stomach.
* Mucus protects the stomach lining.
* The stomach mixes food to form a semi-liquid material called chyme.

### 1.6 Small Intestine

The small intestine is the main site of chemical digestion and nutrient absorption.

It has three major parts:

1. Duodenum.
2. Jejunum.
3. Ileum.

The duodenum receives bile from the liver through the biliary system and pancreatic juice from the pancreas.

The jejunum and ileum absorb most nutrients from digested food.

#### Intestinal Villi

The inner lining of the small intestine contains numerous finger-like projections called villi.

Each villus contains blood capillaries and a lymphatic vessel called a lacteal.

**Functions of villi:**

* Increase the surface area for absorption.
* Absorb glucose and amino acids into blood capillaries.
* Absorb most dietary fats into lymphatic vessels.

**Important:** The large surface area of villi makes nutrient absorption efficient.

### 1.7 Large Intestine

The large intestine includes the caecum, colon and rectum.

Its major functions are:

* Absorption of remaining water and electrolytes.
* Formation of faeces.
* Temporary storage of faeces in the rectum.
* Elimination of faeces through the anus.

The large intestine contains beneficial microorganisms that contribute to intestinal health. Some intestinal bacteria produce vitamin K and certain B vitamins, although the amount available to the body varies.

### 1.8 Associated Digestive Glands

The major associated digestive glands are the salivary glands, liver and pancreas.

#### Liver

The liver is the largest internal gland in the human body.

Important functions include:

* Producing bile.
* Processing absorbed nutrients.
* Storing glycogen.
* Helping regulate blood glucose.
* Detoxifying or metabolizing various substances.
* Producing several blood proteins.

**Bile is produced by the liver and stored and concentrated in the gallbladder.**

Bile contains bile salts that emulsify fats. Emulsification breaks large fat droplets into smaller droplets, increasing the surface area available for lipase action.

**Important:** Bile contains no digestive enzyme.

#### Pancreas

The pancreas has both exocrine and endocrine functions.

Its exocrine function involves secreting digestive enzymes and bicarbonate into the small intestine.

Pancreatic juice contains:

* Pancreatic amylase.
* Pancreatic lipase.
* Protease precursors, including trypsinogen.
* Bicarbonate ions.

Bicarbonate helps neutralize acidic chyme entering the duodenum from the stomach.

The endocrine pancreas contains the islets of Langerhans, which release hormones such as insulin and glucagon.

### 1.9 Digestive Enzymes

| Enzyme             | Main Source                  | Substance Acted Upon  | Main Result                      |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Salivary amylase   | Salivary glands              | Starch                | Smaller carbohydrates            |
| Pepsin             | Stomach                      | Proteins              | Peptides                         |
| Pancreatic amylase | Pancreas                     | Starch                | Smaller carbohydrates            |
| Trypsin            | Activated in small intestine | Proteins and peptides | Smaller peptides                 |
| Pancreatic lipase  | Pancreas                     | Triglycerides         | Fatty acids and monoglycerides   |
| Maltase            | Small-intestinal lining      | Maltose               | Glucose                          |
| Sucrase            | Small-intestinal lining      | Sucrose               | Glucose and fructose             |
| Lactase            | Small-intestinal lining      | Lactose               | Glucose and galactose            |
| Peptidases         | Small-intestinal lining      | Small peptides        | Amino acids and smaller peptides |

### 1.10 Digestion of Carbohydrates, Proteins and Fats

**Carbohydrates:** Digestion begins in the mouth through salivary amylase and continues in the small intestine. The final absorbable products include monosaccharides such as glucose, fructose and galactose.

**Proteins:** Digestion begins mainly in the stomach through pepsin. Pancreatic and intestinal enzymes continue digestion in the small intestine. Amino acids and small peptides are absorbed.

**Fats:** Bile salts emulsify fats. Pancreatic lipase then digests triglycerides into absorbable products, mainly fatty acids and monoglycerides. These are absorbed by intestinal cells and most long-chain dietary fats are transported initially through the lymphatic system.

## 2. HUMAN NUTRITION

### 2.1 Definition

Nutrition is the process by which organisms obtain food and utilize its nutrients for energy, growth, repair and maintenance.

The major nutrients required by humans are:

1. Carbohydrates.
2. Proteins.
3. Fats.
4. Vitamins.
5. Minerals.
6. Water.

Dietary fibre is also important for normal bowel function and digestive health.

### 2.2 Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are an important source of energy.

Examples of carbohydrate-rich foods include rice, wheat, millets, potatoes and fruits.

Types of carbohydrates:

* Monosaccharides: glucose, fructose and galactose.
* Disaccharides: sucrose, lactose and maltose.
* Polysaccharides: starch, glycogen and cellulose.

**Important Facts**

* Glucose is an important energy source for cells.
* Starch is a major storage carbohydrate in plants.
* Glycogen is a storage carbohydrate in humans and other animals.
* Cellulose is a structural component of plant cell walls.
* Humans cannot digest cellulose because they lack the enzyme cellulase.

### 2.3 Proteins

Proteins are required for growth, tissue repair, enzyme formation, hormone production and immune function.

Proteins are made of amino acids.

Sources include:

* Pulses and legumes.
* Milk and dairy products.
* Eggs.
* Fish.
* Meat.
* Soybeans and other protein-rich plant foods.

Some amino acids cannot be synthesized in sufficient amounts by the human body and must be obtained from food. They are called essential amino acids.

### 2.4 Fats

Fats provide concentrated energy and help in the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

Functions of fats:

* Provide energy.
* Form components of cell membranes.
* Protect internal organs.
* Help maintain body temperature.
* Support the absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K.

Dietary fat should include appropriate sources of unsaturated fats. Trans fats should be avoided as far as possible.

### 2.5 Vitamins

Vitamins are organic compounds required in small amounts for normal growth and metabolism.

They are classified into two groups.

**Fat-soluble vitamins:** A, D, E and K.

**Water-soluble vitamins:** Vitamin C and the B-complex vitamins.

| Vitamin     | Major Function                               | Deficiency Effect                                                             |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Vitamin A   | Vision and maintenance of epithelial tissues | Night blindness                                                               |
| Vitamin B1  | Energy metabolism and nerve function         | Beriberi                                                                      |
| Vitamin B2  | Energy metabolism                            | Deficiency may cause cracks at the corners of the mouth and other symptoms    |
| Vitamin B3  | Energy metabolism                            | Pellagra                                                                      |
| Vitamin B9  | DNA synthesis and red blood cell formation   | Megaloblastic anaemia                                                         |
| Vitamin B12 | Nerve function and red blood cell formation  | Megaloblastic anaemia and possible nerve problems                             |
| Vitamin C   | Collagen formation and wound healing         | Scurvy                                                                        |
| Vitamin D   | Calcium absorption and bone health           | Rickets in children; osteomalacia in adults                                   |
| Vitamin E   | Antioxidant function                         | Deficiency can cause neurological or muscle problems in certain circumstances |
| Vitamin K   | Blood clotting                               | Increased bleeding tendency                                                   |

**TNPSC Memory Tip:**
A — Eyes; B1 — Beriberi; B3 — Pellagra; C — Scurvy; D — Rickets; K — Clotting.

### 2.6 Minerals

Minerals are inorganic nutrients required for body structure and physiological functions.

| Mineral    | Function                            | Deficiency or Related Condition                                |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Calcium    | Bones, teeth, muscle contraction    | Weak bones and increased fracture risk                         |
| Iron       | Haemoglobin formation               | Iron-deficiency anaemia                                        |
| Iodine     | Thyroid hormone production          | Iodine-deficiency goitre                                       |
| Phosphorus | Bones, teeth, ATP and nucleic acids | Deficiency is uncommon but can affect bones and muscles        |
| Sodium     | Fluid balance and nerve function    | Severe deficiency can cause hyponatraemia                      |
| Potassium  | Nerve impulses and muscle function  | Low levels can cause muscle weakness and heart rhythm problems |
| Zinc       | Enzymes, growth and immune function | Deficiency can impair growth and wound healing                 |

### 2.7 Balanced Diet

A balanced diet provides adequate energy and essential nutrients in appropriate proportions for an individual's age, activity, health and physiological needs.

A balanced diet generally includes:

* Cereals and millets.
* Pulses and other protein sources.
* Vegetables.
* Fruits.
* Appropriate amounts of milk or alternatives.
* Healthy fat sources.
* Adequate water.

No single food supplies every nutrient in the amounts required by the body.

### 2.8 Malnutrition

Malnutrition includes deficiencies, excesses or imbalances in energy and nutrient intake.

Examples:

* Undernutrition.
* Protein-energy malnutrition.
* Vitamin deficiencies.
* Mineral deficiencies.
* Overnutrition.

Two commonly studied forms of severe childhood protein-energy malnutrition are:

**Kwashiorkor:** Traditionally associated with severe protein deficiency, often with oedema. It usually develops in the context of inadequate overall nutrition.

**Marasmus:** Severe wasting associated with prolonged deficiency of energy and other nutrients.

These conditions require professional medical assessment and appropriate nutritional treatment.

## 3. HUMAN RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

### 3.1 Definition

The respiratory system enables the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide between the body and the external environment.

Oxygen is used by cells during aerobic cellular respiration to release energy from nutrients. Carbon dioxide is produced during cellular metabolism and must be removed from the body.

### 3.2 Parts of the Respiratory System

The major structures are:

1. Nose and nasal cavity.
2. Pharynx.
3. Larynx.
4. Trachea.
5. Bronchi.
6. Bronchioles.
7. Alveoli.
8. Lungs.
9. Diaphragm and other respiratory muscles.

### 3.3 Nose and Nasal Cavity

The nasal cavity:

* Filters inhaled particles.
* Warms incoming air.
* Moistens incoming air.
* Contains receptors involved in smell.

Mucus and cilia help trap and remove particles from the respiratory passages.

### 3.4 Pharynx and Larynx

The pharynx acts as a passage for both food and air.

The larynx contains the vocal cords and is involved in voice production.

The epiglottis helps protect the airway during swallowing by directing food away from the laryngeal opening.

### 3.5 Trachea and Bronchi

The trachea is also called the windpipe.

It contains C-shaped rings of cartilage that help prevent it from collapsing.

The trachea divides into the right and left primary bronchi, which enter the lungs and branch into smaller airways.

The bronchi divide repeatedly into bronchioles.

### 3.6 Alveoli

Alveoli are tiny air sacs located at the ends of respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts.

They are the main sites of gas exchange in the lungs.

Characteristics of alveoli:

* Very thin walls.
* Moist internal surfaces.
* Extensive capillary networks.
* Large total surface area.

Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air into the blood. Carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli and is exhaled.

**TNPSC Important Fact:** Alveoli are the primary sites of gaseous exchange in human lungs.

### 3.7 Mechanism of Breathing

Breathing consists of inspiration and expiration.

#### Inspiration

During quiet inspiration:

1. The diaphragm contracts and moves downward.
2. External intercostal muscles help raise the ribs.
3. The volume of the thoracic cavity increases.
4. Pressure inside the lungs decreases relative to atmospheric pressure.
5. Air flows into the lungs.

#### Expiration

During quiet expiration:

1. The diaphragm relaxes and moves upward.
2. The rib cage returns towards its resting position.
3. Thoracic volume decreases.
4. Pressure inside the lungs rises relative to atmospheric pressure.
5. Air flows out of the lungs.

Quiet expiration is generally passive, whereas forced expiration uses additional muscles.

### 3.8 Difference Between Breathing and Cellular Respiration

| Breathing                                          | Cellular Respiration                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Physical movement of air into and out of the lungs | Biochemical process that releases usable energy from nutrients |
| Involves respiratory muscles and airways           | Involves enzymes and cellular structures                       |
| Includes inspiration and expiration                | May be aerobic or anaerobic                                    |
| Does not itself produce ATP from glucose           | Produces ATP through metabolic pathways                        |

### 3.9 Aerobic Cellular Respiration

The overall simplified equation for aerobic respiration of glucose is:

**Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy (ATP)**

Balanced chemical equation:

**C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy**

Important points:

* Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm.
* In eukaryotic cells, the citric acid cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.
* The electron transport chain is located in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
* Oxygen serves as the final electron acceptor in the mitochondrial electron transport chain.

### 3.10 Transport of Oxygen

Most oxygen in human blood is transported bound to haemoglobin in red blood cells.

A small amount is dissolved in plasma.

Haemoglobin combines reversibly with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin.

Oxygen is released to tissues according to their needs and local conditions.

### 3.11 Transport of Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide is transported in the blood in three principal forms:

1. Mainly as bicarbonate ions.
2. Bound to proteins, including haemoglobin.
3. Dissolved in plasma.

Most carbon dioxide is transported as bicarbonate.

In red blood cells, the enzyme carbonic anhydrase speeds up the conversion of carbon dioxide and water into carbonic acid, which dissociates into hydrogen ions and bicarbonate ions.

### 3.12 Respiratory Rate and Lung Volumes

Respiratory rate is the number of breaths taken per minute.

It varies with age, physical activity, sleep, illness and other conditions.

Important lung-volume terms:

* **Tidal volume:** Air inhaled or exhaled during a normal quiet breath.
* **Inspiratory reserve volume:** Additional air that can be inhaled after a normal inspiration.
* **Expiratory reserve volume:** Additional air that can be exhaled after a normal expiration.
* **Residual volume:** Air remaining in the lungs after maximal expiration.
* **Vital capacity:** Maximum amount of air exhaled after a maximal inspiration.

**Formula:**

Vital Capacity = Tidal Volume + Inspiratory Reserve Volume + Expiratory Reserve Volume.

Residual volume is not included in vital capacity.

### 3.13 Common Respiratory Disorders

**Asthma:** A condition involving inflammation and narrowing of the airways, which can cause wheezing, coughing and breathing difficulty.

**Pneumonia:** An infection that inflames the air sacs of one or both lungs.

**Tuberculosis:** An infectious disease caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. It most commonly affects the lungs but can affect other organs.

**Emphysema:** A condition involving damage to alveolar walls and reduced efficiency of gas exchange. It is commonly associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

**Important:** Smoking and exposure to harmful airborne pollutants increase the risk of several respiratory diseases.

# PART 3: BLOOD, CIRCULATORY SYSTEM, HUMAN HEART AND EXCRETION

# CHAPTER 1: BLOOD

## 1.1 Introduction to Blood

Blood is a specialized fluid connective tissue that circulates throughout the human body. It transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste products between different organs and tissues.

Blood performs several essential functions necessary for maintaining life.

### Major Functions of Blood

1. **Transportation:** Carries oxygen from the lungs to body tissues.
2. **Nutrient transport:** Carries absorbed nutrients to cells.
3. **Waste removal:** Transports carbon dioxide and metabolic waste products to organs responsible for their elimination.
4. **Hormone transport:** Carries hormones from endocrine glands to target organs.
5. **Protection:** White blood cells help protect the body against infections.
6. **Blood clotting:** Platelets and clotting proteins help prevent excessive bleeding.
7. **Temperature regulation:** Distributes heat throughout the body.
8. **pH regulation:** Helps maintain the body's acid–base balance.
9. **Fluid balance:** Helps maintain the distribution of water between blood and tissues.

Blood is slightly alkaline, with a normal arterial blood pH of approximately 7.35–7.45.

An average adult has approximately 4.5–6 litres of blood, although the amount varies with body size and other factors.

## 1.2 Composition of Blood

Blood consists of two major components:

1. Plasma.
2. Formed elements.

### A. Plasma

Plasma is the liquid component of blood.

It constitutes approximately 55% of blood volume under typical conditions.

Plasma is mainly composed of water, together with proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones and waste products.

**Major components of plasma:**

* Water.
* Albumin.
* Globulins.
* Fibrinogen.
* Electrolytes.
* Glucose.
* Amino acids.
* Hormones.
* Dissolved gases.
* Metabolic waste products.

#### Important Plasma Proteins

**Albumin:**

* Helps maintain the osmotic pressure of blood.
* Contributes to fluid balance between blood and tissues.
* Transports several substances.

**Globulins:**

* Include proteins involved in immunity.
* Some globulins transport substances through the blood.

**Fibrinogen:**

* Plays an important role in blood clotting.
* Is converted into fibrin during clot formation.

**TNPSC Important Fact:** Plasma contains fibrinogen, whereas serum is the fluid remaining after blood has clotted and therefore lacks fibrinogen consumed during clotting.

### B. Formed Elements

The formed elements of blood are:

1. Red blood cells.
2. White blood cells.
3. Platelets.

These components perform different functions in the body.

---

## 1.3 Red Blood Cells (RBCs)

Red blood cells are also called erythrocytes.

Their primary function is to transport oxygen from the lungs to body tissues.

### Characteristics of RBCs

* Mature human RBCs normally lack a nucleus.
* They contain haemoglobin.
* They have a biconcave disc shape.
* Their shape provides a large surface area relative to volume.
* They are flexible enough to pass through narrow capillaries.
* They are produced in red bone marrow.

### Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is an iron-containing protein present in red blood cells.

It binds oxygen reversibly and transports it through the bloodstream.

Haemoglobin also contributes to the transport of carbon dioxide and the buffering of blood.

**Important Facts**

* RBCs are also called erythrocytes.
* Haemoglobin contains iron.
* Iron is essential for haemoglobin formation.
* Mature human RBCs lack a nucleus.
* The average lifespan of a circulating human RBC is approximately 120 days.
* Old RBCs are removed mainly by macrophages in the spleen and liver.

### Erythropoiesis

Erythropoiesis is the process of red blood cell formation.

In adults, it takes place mainly in red bone marrow.

The hormone erythropoietin stimulates RBC production when oxygen delivery to tissues is insufficient. It is produced mainly by the kidneys.

### Anaemia

Anaemia is a condition in which the haemoglobin concentration or the number of healthy red blood cells is insufficient for normal oxygen delivery.

Possible causes include:

* Iron deficiency.
* Vitamin B12 deficiency.
* Folate deficiency.
* Blood loss.
* Certain inherited disorders.
* Chronic diseases.

**TNPSC Point:** Iron deficiency is a common cause of nutritional anaemia.

---

## 1.4 White Blood Cells (WBCs)

White blood cells are also called leukocytes.

Their main function is to defend the body against infections and other harmful agents.

Unlike mature human RBCs, WBCs have nuclei.

### Types of White Blood Cells

The five major types of circulating white blood cells are:

1. Neutrophils.
2. Lymphocytes.
3. Monocytes.
4. Eosinophils.
5. Basophils.

### A. Neutrophils

* Usually the most abundant type of circulating WBC in healthy adults.
* Help defend against bacterial and fungal infections.
* Can engulf microorganisms through phagocytosis.

### B. Lymphocytes

Lymphocytes include B cells, T cells and natural killer cells.

**B lymphocytes:**

* Can develop into plasma cells.
* Plasma cells produce antibodies.

**T lymphocytes:**

* Participate in cell-mediated immunity.
* Some directly attack infected cells.
* Helper T cells coordinate immune responses.

**Natural killer cells:**

* Help destroy certain virus-infected cells and abnormal cells.

### C. Monocytes

* Circulate in blood.
* Can enter tissues and differentiate into macrophages or related cells.
* Participate in phagocytosis and immune responses.

### D. Eosinophils

* Participate in defence against certain parasites.
* Are involved in allergic and inflammatory responses.

### E. Basophils

* Release substances such as histamine.
* Participate in allergic and inflammatory reactions.

### Important Comparison

| WBC Type    | Major Function                                                     |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Neutrophils | Phagocytosis; defence against many bacterial and fungal infections |
| Lymphocytes | Adaptive immunity and other immune functions                       |
| Monocytes   | Phagocytosis and development into tissue immune cells              |
| Eosinophils | Defence against certain parasites; allergic responses              |
| Basophils   | Release mediators involved in inflammation and allergy             |

**TNPSC Memory Tip:** Neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils are the five major types of circulating WBCs.

---

## 1.5 Platelets

Platelets are also called thrombocytes.

They are small cell fragments produced from large bone marrow cells called megakaryocytes.

### Functions of Platelets

1. Help form the initial platelet plug at an injured blood vessel.
2. Participate in blood clotting.
3. Release substances that support clot formation.
4. Help maintain the integrity of damaged blood vessels.

### Thrombocytopenia

Thrombocytopenia is a condition involving a lower-than-normal platelet count.

It can increase the risk of bleeding.

### Blood Clotting

Blood clotting, also called coagulation, prevents excessive blood loss following blood vessel injury.

A simplified sequence is:

1. A blood vessel is injured.
2. Platelets adhere to the damaged area.
3. Platelets aggregate to form a temporary plug.
4. Clotting factors activate a cascade of reactions.
5. Thrombin converts fibrinogen into fibrin.
6. Fibrin forms a mesh that strengthens the clot.

**TNPSC Important Fact:** Fibrinogen is a soluble plasma protein; fibrin forms the structural mesh of a blood clot.

# CHAPTER 2: BLOOD GROUPS

## 2.1 ABO Blood Group System

The ABO blood group system is based on the presence or absence of A and B antigens on red blood cells.

The major blood groups are A, B, AB and O.

| Blood Group | RBC Antigens            | Naturally Occurring Plasma Antibodies |
| ----------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| A           | A antigen               | Anti-B                                |
| B           | B antigen               | Anti-A                                |
| AB          | A and B antigens        | Neither anti-A nor anti-B             |
| O           | Neither A nor B antigen | Anti-A and anti-B                     |

This table describes the usual ABO pattern.

Actual blood transfusions require compatibility testing and professional laboratory procedures.

## 2.2 Rh Blood Group System

The Rh blood group system contains several antigens. The D antigen is particularly important in routine blood grouping.

* Rh-positive: D antigen is present.
* Rh-negative: D antigen is absent.

An Rh-negative person can develop anti-D antibodies after exposure to Rh-positive red blood cells.

Rh incompatibility can be medically important during pregnancy.

### Rh Incompatibility in Pregnancy

If an Rh-negative pregnant person carries an Rh-positive fetus, fetal red blood cells entering the maternal circulation may trigger the production of anti-D antibodies.

In a later pregnancy, these antibodies can cross the placenta and destroy fetal red blood cells if the fetus is Rh-positive.

Appropriate medical care, including anti-D immunoglobulin when indicated, can prevent many cases of Rh sensitization.

### Important Facts

* ABO is a major blood group system.
* The D antigen is important in the Rh system.
* Blood transfusions require compatibility testing.
* Anti-D immunoglobulin can help prevent Rh sensitization in appropriate clinical circumstances.

# CHAPTER 3: HUMAN CIRCULATORY SYSTEM

## 3.1 Introduction

The human circulatory system is also called the cardiovascular system.

It consists mainly of:

1. Heart.
2. Blood.
3. Blood vessels.

Its main function is to circulate blood throughout the body.

### Major Functions

* Delivers oxygen to tissues.
* Transports nutrients.
* Carries hormones.
* Removes carbon dioxide.
* Transports metabolic waste.
* Supports immunity.
* Helps regulate body temperature.
* Maintains fluid balance.

Humans have a closed circulatory system because blood normally remains within blood vessels.

Humans also have a double circulatory system because blood passes through the heart twice during one complete circuit through the pulmonary and systemic circulations.

---

## 3.2 Types of Blood Vessels

The three major types of blood vessels are arteries, veins and capillaries.

### A. Arteries

Arteries carry blood away from the heart.

Characteristics:

* Usually have thick, elastic and muscular walls.
* Carry blood under relatively high pressure.
* Have a relatively narrow lumen compared with many veins.
* Normally do not contain valves along their main length.

Most arteries carry oxygen-rich blood, but the pulmonary arteries carry oxygen-poor blood from the right ventricle to the lungs.

### B. Veins

Veins carry blood towards the heart.

Characteristics:

* Usually have thinner walls than comparable arteries.
* Often have a larger lumen.
* Many contain valves that help prevent backflow.
* Carry blood under relatively low pressure.

Most veins carry oxygen-poor blood, but the pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood from the lungs to the left atrium.

### C. Capillaries

Capillaries are microscopic blood vessels connecting the arterial and venous sides of the circulation.

Their walls are extremely thin, generally consisting of a single layer of endothelial cells.

Functions:

* Exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide.
* Exchange nutrients and waste products.
* Allow movement of water and certain dissolved substances between blood and tissues.

### Comparison of Blood Vessels

| Feature       | Arteries                           | Veins                | Capillaries                      |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Direction     | Away from heart                    | Towards heart        | Connect small arteries and veins |
| Wall          | Thick and muscular                 | Thinner              | Very thin                        |
| Pressure      | Relatively high                    | Relatively low       | Low compared with arteries       |
| Valves        | Generally absent along main length | Common in many veins | Absent                           |
| Main function | Distribution of blood              | Return of blood      | Exchange of substances           |

**Remember:** Arteries and veins are classified by the direction in which blood travels, not by oxygen content.

# CHAPTER 4: HUMAN HEART

## 4.1 Structure of the Heart

The human heart is a muscular organ located in the thoracic cavity between the lungs, slightly towards the left.

It is enclosed by a protective covering called the pericardium.

The heart has four chambers:

1. Right atrium.
2. Right ventricle.
3. Left atrium.
4. Left ventricle.

### Right Atrium

* Receives oxygen-poor blood from the body.
* Blood enters mainly through the superior and inferior venae cavae.
* Also receives blood from the coronary circulation through the coronary sinus.

### Right Ventricle

* Receives blood from the right atrium.
* Pumps oxygen-poor blood to the lungs through the pulmonary trunk and pulmonary arteries.

### Left Atrium

* Receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs through the pulmonary veins.

### Left Ventricle

* Receives blood from the left atrium.
* Pumps oxygen-rich blood into the aorta.
* Has the thickest muscular wall among the four chambers because it pumps blood throughout the systemic circulation.

## 4.2 Heart Valves

The heart contains valves that help maintain one-way blood flow.

The four major valves are:

1. Tricuspid valve.
2. Pulmonary valve.
3. Mitral valve.
4. Aortic valve.

### Tricuspid Valve

Located between the right atrium and right ventricle.

It helps prevent backflow into the right atrium when the right ventricle contracts.

### Pulmonary Valve

Located between the right ventricle and pulmonary trunk.

It prevents backflow from the pulmonary trunk into the right ventricle.

### Mitral Valve

Also called the bicuspid valve.

Located between the left atrium and left ventricle.

### Aortic Valve

Located between the left ventricle and aorta.

It prevents backflow from the aorta into the left ventricle.

### Important Facts

* Tricuspid valve: right atrioventricular valve.
* Mitral valve: left atrioventricular valve.
* Pulmonary and aortic valves are semilunar valves.
* Heart valves help maintain one-way blood flow.

## 4.4 Double Circulation

Double circulation means that blood passes through the heart twice during one complete circuit.

It consists of pulmonary circulation and systemic circulation.

### A. Pulmonary Circulation

Pulmonary circulation carries blood between the heart and lungs.

Sequence:

Right ventricle → Pulmonary arteries → Lung capillaries → Pulmonary veins → Left atrium.

Functions:

* Delivers oxygen-poor blood to the lungs.
* Allows carbon dioxide to leave the blood.
* Allows oxygen to enter the blood.
* Returns oxygen-rich blood to the heart.

### B. Systemic Circulation

Systemic circulation carries blood between the heart and the rest of the body.

Sequence:

Left ventricle → Aorta → Body tissues → Venae cavae → Right atrium.

Functions:

* Supplies oxygen and nutrients to body tissues.
* Collects carbon dioxide and other waste products.
* Returns blood to the heart.

### Advantages of Double Circulation

* Helps maintain efficient oxygen delivery.
* Keeps pulmonary and systemic circulation functionally separate.
* Allows systemic blood to be pumped at a pressure suitable for body tissues.

---

## 4.5 Cardiac Cycle

The cardiac cycle refers to the sequence of events occurring during one heartbeat.

It includes atrial contraction, ventricular contraction and relaxation.

### Atrial Systole

The atria contract and push blood into the ventricles.

### Ventricular Systole

The ventricles contract.

* The right ventricle pumps blood towards the lungs.
* The left ventricle pumps blood into the aorta.

### Diastole

The heart muscle relaxes, allowing the chambers to fill with blood.

Atrial and ventricular relaxation occur during the cardiac cycle.

### Heart Sounds

The two main heart sounds are commonly described as “lub” and “dub.”

* **First heart sound (S1):** Mainly associated with closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves.
* **Second heart sound (S2):** Mainly associated with closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves.

---

## 4.6 Heart Rate and Cardiac Output

Heart rate is the number of heartbeats per minute.

Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute.

**Formula:**

Cardiac Output = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume.

For example, if the heart rate is 70 beats per minute and stroke volume is 70 mL per beat:

Cardiac output = 70 × 70

Cardiac output = 4,900 mL/minute

Therefore, cardiac output is approximately 4.9 litres per minute.

This is an illustrative calculation; actual cardiac output varies with the person's condition and activity.

### Pulse

The pulse is the rhythmic expansion of an artery caused by pressure waves generated by heart contractions.

The pulse rate generally corresponds closely to heart rate when the rhythm is regular.

---

## 4.7 Electrical Conduction System of the Heart

The heartbeat is coordinated by specialized cardiac tissue.

Major components include:

1. Sinoatrial node.
2. Atrioventricular node.
3. Atrioventricular bundle.
4. Right and left bundle branches.
5. Purkinje fibres.

### Sinoatrial Node

The sinoatrial node, or SA node, is the normal pacemaker of the heart.

It initiates electrical impulses that normally determine the heart's rhythm.

### Atrioventricular Node

The atrioventricular node, or AV node, receives impulses from the atria and briefly delays their transmission before they travel towards the ventricles.

### Bundle of His and Purkinje Fibres

These structures conduct electrical impulses through the ventricles and coordinate ventricular contraction.

**TNPSC Important Fact:** The SA node is the normal pacemaker of the human heart.

---

# CHAPTER 5: BLOOD PRESSURE AND COMMON CARDIOVASCULAR CONDITIONS

## 5.1 Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is the pressure exerted by circulating blood on the walls of blood vessels.

It is commonly measured using two values.

### Systolic Pressure

The pressure during ventricular contraction.

### Diastolic Pressure

The pressure during ventricular relaxation.

Blood pressure is commonly expressed in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).

A reading of 120/80 mmHg is often used as a familiar reference example, but normal and abnormal categories depend on clinical guidelines, measurement conditions and individual circumstances.

## 5.2 Hypertension

Hypertension is persistently elevated blood pressure.

Risk factors may include:

* High salt intake.
* Physical inactivity.
* Excessive body weight.
* Family history.
* Increasing age.
* Certain kidney and endocrine conditions.

Long-term uncontrolled hypertension increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke and kidney damage.

## 5.3 Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is the accumulation of plaques containing lipids, inflammatory cells and other materials within the walls of arteries.

It can narrow arteries and reduce blood flow.

It is associated with coronary artery disease and some forms of stroke.

## 5.4 Coronary Artery Disease

The coronary arteries supply oxygen-rich blood to the heart muscle.

Coronary artery disease develops when these arteries become narrowed or obstructed, commonly because of atherosclerosis.

Reduced blood supply to the heart muscle can cause angina.

A heart attack occurs when blood flow to part of the heart muscle is severely reduced or blocked, causing injury to the tissue.

## 5.5 Stroke

A stroke occurs when part of the brain is damaged because its blood supply is interrupted or because bleeding occurs within or around the brain.

Major types include:

* Ischaemic stroke.
* Haemorrhagic stroke.

Stroke is a medical emergency.

## 5.6 Lymphatic System

The lymphatic system consists of lymph, lymphatic vessels, lymph nodes and lymphatic organs.

### Functions

* Returns excess tissue fluid to the bloodstream.
* Transports certain dietary fats from the small intestine.
* Supports immune defence.
* Provides sites for immune-cell activation.

### Lymph

Lymph is a fluid that flows through lymphatic vessels.

It is formed from tissue fluid entering lymphatic capillaries.

### Lymph Nodes

Lymph nodes filter lymph and contain immune cells that help detect and respond to pathogens.

**TNPSC Point:** The lymphatic system helps maintain fluid balance and supports immunity.

# CHAPTER 6: EXCRETORY SYSTEM

## 6.1 Introduction to Excretion

Excretion is the process of removing metabolic waste products from the body.

Examples of metabolic waste include:

* Urea.
* Carbon dioxide.
* Excess water.
* Excess salts.
* Certain metabolic by-products.

Excretion is different from egestion.

**Excretion:** Removal of metabolic waste produced by the body's cells.

**Egestion:** Removal of undigested food from the digestive tract as faeces.

### Major Excretory Organs

**Kidneys:** Remove nitrogenous wastes and help regulate water, electrolytes and acid–base balance.

**Lungs:** Remove carbon dioxide and water vapour.

**Skin:** Sweat glands remove water, salts and small amounts of other substances.

**Liver:** Processes many substances, converts ammonia into urea and helps metabolize or eliminate various compounds through bile.

---

## 6.2 Human Urinary System

The human urinary system consists of:

1. Two kidneys.
2. Two ureters.
3. Urinary bladder.
4. Urethra.

### Kidneys

The kidneys are bean-shaped organs located towards the back of the abdominal cavity, one on each side of the vertebral column.

Major functions:

* Filter blood plasma.
* Excrete urea and other waste products.
* Regulate water balance.
* Regulate electrolyte concentrations.
* Help maintain blood pH.
* Participate in blood pressure regulation.
* Produce erythropoietin.
* Activate vitamin D into its biologically active form.

### Ureters

The ureters carry urine from the kidneys to the urinary bladder.

They use smooth-muscle contractions to move urine.

### Urinary Bladder

The urinary bladder temporarily stores urine.

Its muscular wall contracts during urination.

### Urethra

The urethra carries urine from the bladder to the outside of the body.

---

# CHAPTER 7: STRUCTURE OF THE KIDNEY

## 7.1 External Structure

Each kidney has an outer covering and internal regions.

The main internal regions are:

1. Renal cortex.
2. Renal medulla.
3. Renal pelvis.

### Renal Cortex

The renal cortex is the outer region of the kidney.

It contains renal corpuscles and parts of the kidney tubules.

### Renal Medulla

The renal medulla is the inner region.

It contains structures such as loops of Henle and collecting ducts, which contribute to the formation of concentrated or dilute urine.

### Renal Pelvis

The renal pelvis collects urine and directs it into the ureter.

---

## 7.2 Nephron

The nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney.

Each nephron filters blood and modifies the filtrate to form urine.

A nephron consists of:

1. Renal corpuscle.
2. Renal tubule.

### Renal Corpuscle

The renal corpuscle consists of:

* Glomerulus.
* Bowman's capsule.

**Glomerulus:** A network of capillaries where blood filtration begins.

**Bowman's capsule:** A cup-shaped structure surrounding the glomerulus that receives the filtrate.

### Renal Tubule

The renal tubule consists of:

1. Proximal convoluted tubule.
2. Loop of Henle.
3. Distal convoluted tubule.

The collecting ducts receive fluid from multiple nephrons and carry it towards the renal pelvis.

### Important Facts

* Nephron is the functional unit of the kidney.
* Glomerular filtration occurs in the renal corpuscle.
* The proximal convoluted tubule reabsorbs much of the filtered water and solutes.
* The loop of Henle helps establish the medullary concentration gradient.
* The distal nephron contributes to electrolyte and acid–base regulation.
* Collecting ducts contribute to final urine concentration.

---

# CHAPTER 8: FORMATION OF URINE

Urine formation involves three major processes:

1. Glomerular filtration.
2. Selective tubular reabsorption.
3. Tubular secretion.

## 8.1 Glomerular Filtration

Blood enters the glomerulus through the afferent arteriole.

Pressure drives water and small dissolved substances through the filtration barrier into Bowman's capsule.

Normally, blood cells and most large plasma proteins remain in the bloodstream.

The fluid formed is called glomerular filtrate.

It contains water, electrolytes, glucose, amino acids and nitrogenous waste products, among other small molecules.

## 8.2 Selective Tubular Reabsorption

During tubular reabsorption, useful substances move from the tubular fluid back into the blood.

These substances include:

* Glucose.
* Amino acids.
* Water.
* Sodium.
* Bicarbonate.
* Other required ions.

Under normal conditions, nearly all filtered glucose is reabsorbed when the filtered load is within the kidneys' reabsorptive capacity.

## 8.3 Tubular Secretion

During tubular secretion, certain substances move from the blood into the tubular fluid.

These may include:

* Hydrogen ions.
* Potassium ions.
* Ammonium.
* Certain drugs and metabolites.

Secretion helps regulate acid–base balance, potassium balance and the elimination of certain substances.

## 8.4 Concentration of Urine

The kidneys regulate how much water is retained or excreted.

The loop of Henle and collecting ducts contribute to the formation of concentrated urine.

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH), also called vasopressin, increases water permeability in parts of the collecting ducts, helping the body conserve water.

When ADH activity is high, more water is generally reabsorbed and urine becomes more concentrated.

When ADH activity is low, less water is reabsorbed in the collecting ducts and urine is generally more dilute.

---

# CHAPTER 9: NITROGENOUS WASTE PRODUCTS

Nitrogenous wastes arise mainly from the metabolism of proteins and nucleic acids.

The major nitrogenous excretory products studied in biology are ammonia, urea and uric acid.

## 9.1 Ammonia

Ammonia is highly toxic, especially when it accumulates in body fluids.

Many aquatic animals excrete ammonia directly because water is readily available for dilution.

Such animals are called ammonotelic animals.

Examples include many bony fishes and aquatic invertebrates.

## 9.2 Urea

Urea is produced mainly in the liver through the urea cycle.

The process converts toxic ammonia into a less toxic, water-soluble compound that can be transported in blood to the kidneys.

Humans are ureotelic animals because urea is the major nitrogenous waste excreted in urine.

Examples of ureotelic animals include humans and many other mammals.

## 9.3 Uric Acid

Uric acid is relatively poorly soluble in water.

Its excretion requires comparatively little water.

Birds, many reptiles and insects are commonly described as uricotelic.

### Comparison

| Waste Product | Relative Toxicity                             | Water Requirement for Excretion | Common Examples                  |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Ammonia       | High                                          | High                            | Many aquatic animals             |
| Urea          | Lower than ammonia                            | Moderate                        | Mammals, including humans        |
| Uric acid     | Relatively low at normal excretory conditions | Low                             | Birds, many reptiles and insects |

**TNPSC Memory Tip:**

* Ammonotelic — ammonia.
* Ureotelic — urea.
* Uricotelic — uric acid.

# CHAPTER 10: HORMONAL REGULATION OF KIDNEY FUNCTION

## 10.1 Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)

ADH is synthesized in the hypothalamus and released into the bloodstream from the posterior pituitary.

Its major kidney-related function is to increase water reabsorption in the collecting ducts.

When the body needs to conserve water, ADH secretion generally increases.

## 10.2 Aldosterone

Aldosterone is produced by the adrenal cortex.

It acts on parts of the distal nephron to increase sodium reabsorption and promote potassium secretion.

Water retention can increase when sodium is retained, depending on the body's overall physiological state.

## 10.3 Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System

The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, or RAAS, helps regulate blood pressure, blood volume and sodium balance.

A simplified sequence is:

1. The kidneys release renin in response to certain physiological signals.
2. Renin acts on angiotensinogen to produce angiotensin I.
3. Angiotensin-converting enzyme helps convert angiotensin I into angiotensin II.
4. Angiotensin II constricts blood vessels and stimulates aldosterone release.
5. Aldosterone promotes sodium reabsorption in the kidneys.

Together, these mechanisms help maintain blood pressure and fluid balance.

## 10.4 Atrial Natriuretic Peptide

Atrial natriuretic peptide, or ANP, is released by the atria of the heart in response to increased stretching.

It promotes sodium excretion and can oppose certain actions of RAAS.

It contributes to the regulation of blood volume and blood pressure.

# CHAPTER 11: COMMON URINARY SYSTEM DISORDERS

## 11.1 Kidney Stones

Kidney stones are hard deposits that form from minerals and salts in the urinary system.

Their symptoms may include severe pain, blood in urine or urinary problems.

Risk factors depend on stone type and can include inadequate fluid intake and certain metabolic conditions.

## 11.2 Urinary Tract Infection

A urinary tract infection, or UTI, can affect the bladder, urethra or kidneys.

Common symptoms may include painful urination, frequent urination and lower abdominal discomfort.

Kidney infection can cause fever and back or flank pain and requires prompt medical evaluation.

## 11.3 Nephritis

Nephritis refers to inflammation involving kidney tissues.

Depending on the type, it can affect filtration and kidney function.

## 11.4 Renal Failure

Renal failure occurs when the kidneys cannot adequately perform their functions.

It can cause the accumulation of waste products and disturbances in fluid, electrolyte and acid–base balance.

Severe kidney failure may require dialysis or kidney transplantation.

## 11.5 Dialysis

Dialysis is a treatment that removes waste products and excess fluid from the blood when the kidneys cannot adequately do so.

Two main types are:

**Haemodialysis:** Blood passes through a dialysis machine containing a membrane that allows selected substances to move between blood and dialysis fluid.

**Peritoneal dialysis:** The peritoneal membrane inside the abdomen acts as the exchange membrane. Dialysis fluid is introduced into the abdominal cavity and later drained.

Dialysis supports kidney function but does not reproduce every function of healthy kidneys.

## 11.6 Artificial Kidney

An artificial kidney, in the context of haemodialysis, is a device that removes selected waste products and excess water from the blood.

It uses a semipermeable membrane and dialysis fluid to facilitate exchange.

**TNPSC Point:** Dialysis is used when the kidneys cannot adequately remove waste products and excess fluid.
