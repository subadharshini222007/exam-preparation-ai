
PART A - UNIT 1: WEATHER AND CLIMATE OF INDIA

1. INTRODUCTION

Weather refers to the atmospheric condition of a place at a particular time.
Climate refers to the average weather conditions of a place over a long period,
usually 30 years or more.

India has a tropical monsoon type of climate. It is characterised by:
- High temperature during most parts of the year
- Seasonal reversal of winds
- Uneven distribution of rainfall
- Distinct wet and dry seasons

2. FACTORS AFFECTING CLIMATE OF INDIA

2.1 Latitude

India lies between 8°4' N and 37°6' N latitudes.
The Tropic of Cancer passes through the middle of India.
Southern India lies in the tropical zone and remains warm throughout the year.
Northern India lies in the subtropical zone and experiences greater seasonal variation.

2.2 Altitude

Temperature decreases with increase in altitude.
Himalayan regions remain cold throughout the year.
Plains experience higher temperatures.

2.3 Distance from the Sea

Coastal areas have moderate climate due to the influence of the sea.
Interior areas experience extreme climate.
Example: Mumbai has moderate climate, while Delhi has extreme climate.

2.4 Himalayan Mountains

The Himalayas act as a climatic barrier.
They prevent cold Central Asian winds from entering India.
They also help in the formation of the monsoon.

2.5 Western Disturbances

Western disturbances are extra-tropical storms originating in the Mediterranean region.
They bring winter rainfall to north-west India.
They are important for rabi crops, especially wheat.

2.6 El Nino and La Nina

El Nino refers to the warming of sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern
Pacific Ocean.
It generally weakens the Indian monsoon and reduces rainfall.

La Nina refers to the cooling of sea surface temperatures in the same region.
It generally strengthens the Indian monsoon and increases rainfall.

2.7 Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)

IOD refers to the difference in sea surface temperature between the western and eastern
Indian Ocean.
Positive IOD generally supports good monsoon rainfall in India.
Negative IOD generally weakens monsoon rainfall.

3. SEASONS OF INDIA

India has four major seasons:

1. Cold weather season - December to February
2. Hot weather season - March to May
3. Southwest monsoon season - June to September
4. Retreating monsoon season - October to November

3.1 Cold Weather Season

- December to February
- Temperature decreases from south to north
- Western disturbances bring rainfall in north-west India
- Useful for rabi crops

3.2 Hot Weather Season

- March to May
- Temperature rises sharply
- Loo winds blow in north India
- Thunderstorms occur in Kerala, Karnataka and Assam

3.3 Southwest Monsoon Season

- June to September
- Major rainfall season of India
- Winds blow from sea to land
- Kerala receives rainfall first around 1 June

3.4 Retreating Monsoon Season

- October to November
- Winds retreat from north to south
- Tamil Nadu receives most of its rainfall during this season
- Cyclones are common in the Bay of Bengal

4. DISTRIBUTION OF RAINFALL

Areas of high rainfall:
- Western Ghats
- North-East India
- West Bengal
- Coastal Odisha
- Parts of Himachal Pradesh

Areas of low rainfall:
- Western Rajasthan
- Kutch region
- Parts of Deccan Plateau
- Ladakh region

Mawsynram in Meghalaya receives the highest rainfall in India.

5. IMPORTANT EXAM POINTS

- India has tropical monsoon climate
- Tropic of Cancer passes through India
- Himalayas protect India from cold winds
- Western disturbances bring winter rainfall
- El Nino weakens monsoon
- La Nina strengthens monsoon
- Mawsynram receives highest rainfall
- Tamil Nadu receives rainfall during retreating monsoon
- Southwest monsoon begins in Kerala around 1 June

PART B - UNIT 2: INDIAN MONSOON - MECHANISM

1. MEANING OF MONSOON

The word monsoon is derived from the Arabic word 'mausim', meaning season.
Monsoon refers to seasonal reversal of winds.

In summer, winds blow from sea to land.
In winter, winds blow from land to sea.

2. MECHANISM OF INDIAN MONSOON

The Indian monsoon is caused by the combined effect of:

1. Differential heating of land and sea
2. Shift of the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)
3. Tibetan Plateau heating
4. Jet streams
5. Indian Ocean Dipole
6. El Nino and La Nina

2.1 Differential Heating of Land and Sea

During summer, the Indian landmass heats up faster than the surrounding seas.
This creates low pressure over north-west India.
The Indian Ocean remains relatively cooler and has high pressure.
Air moves from high pressure to low pressure.
Thus, moisture-laden winds move from the Indian Ocean towards the Indian landmass.

2.2 Shift of ITCZ

ITCZ means Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone.
It is a low pressure zone near the equator where trade winds converge.

During summer, ITCZ shifts northward.
In July, it lies over the Gangetic plain, around 20°N to 25°N.
This shifted ITCZ is called the monsoon trough.

Due to this shift, southeast trade winds of the Southern Hemisphere cross the equator.
They are deflected to the right due to the Coriolis force.
Thus, they become southwest monsoon winds. [46][47][48]

2.3 Tibetan Plateau Heating

During summer, the Tibetan Plateau gets intensely heated.
This creates a strong low pressure area over Tibet.
This low pressure helps in drawing monsoon winds towards India.

2.4 Jet Streams

Jet streams are fast moving air currents in the upper atmosphere.

Two important jet streams affect Indian monsoon:

1. Subtropical Westerly Jet Stream
2. Tropical Easterly Jet Stream

During winter, the subtropical westerly jet stream flows south of the Himalayas.
During summer, it shifts north of the Himalayas.
This shift helps in the onset of southwest monsoon.

The tropical easterly jet stream develops over the Indian Peninsula during summer.
It helps in strengthening the monsoon.

2.5 Indian Ocean Dipole

Positive IOD:
- Western Indian Ocean becomes warmer
- Eastern Indian Ocean becomes cooler
- This supports good monsoon rainfall in India

Negative IOD:
- Western Indian Ocean becomes cooler
- Eastern Indian Ocean becomes warmer
- This weakens monsoon rainfall in India

2.6 El Nino and La Nina

El Nino:
- Warming of central and eastern Pacific Ocean
- Weakens Indian monsoon
- Reduces rainfall
- May cause drought-like conditions

La Nina:
- Cooling of central and eastern Pacific Ocean
- Strengthens Indian monsoon
- Increases rainfall
- May cause floods in some regions

3. ONSET OF SOUTHWEST MONSOON

- Southwest monsoon normally sets in over Kerala around 1 June
- It reaches Mumbai around 10 June
- It reaches Delhi around 1 July
- It covers the entire country by mid-July

4. BRANCHES OF SOUTHWEST MONSOON

Southwest monsoon has two branches:

1. Arabian Sea Branch
2. Bay of Bengal Branch

4.1 Arabian Sea Branch

- Strikes the Western Ghats
- Causes heavy rainfall in Kerala, coastal Karnataka, Konkan and Mumbai
- Western Ghats receive heavy orographic rainfall

4.2 Bay of Bengal Branch

- Moves towards Assam and North-East India
- Then moves westward along the Himalayas
- Causes rainfall in the Gangetic plain
- Gives rainfall to Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Punjab

5. WITHDRAWAL OF MONSOON

- Withdrawal begins from north-west India in early September
- It withdraws from most parts of India by October
- It withdraws from Tamil Nadu by early December

6. IMPORTANT FACTS

- Monsoon is a seasonal wind system
- ITCZ shifts northward in summer
- Monsoon trough lies over Gangetic plain in July
- Coriolis force deflects winds to the right in Northern Hemisphere
- Southwest monsoon has two branches
- Arabian Sea branch strikes Western Ghats
- Bay of Bengal branch affects North-East India and Gangetic plain
- El Nino weakens monsoon
- La Nina strengthens monsoon
- Positive IOD supports good monsoon
- Negative IOD weakens monsoon

PART A - UNIT 3: SOUTHWEST MONSOON

1. INTRODUCTION

Southwest monsoon is the main rainy season of India.
It occurs from June to September.
It is also called advancing monsoon.

Southwest monsoon contributes about 75% to 90% of India's annual rainfall.

2. ONSET OF SOUTHWEST MONSOON

- Southwest monsoon normally sets in over Kerala around 1 June
- It reaches Mumbai around 10 June
- It reaches Kolkata around 15 June
- It reaches Delhi around 1 July
- It covers the entire country by mid-July

3. BRANCHES OF SOUTHWEST MONSOON

Southwest monsoon has two branches:

1. Arabian Sea Branch
2. Bay of Bengal Branch

3.1 Arabian Sea Branch

- Strikes the Western Ghats
- Causes heavy rainfall in Kerala, coastal Karnataka, Konkan and Mumbai
- Western Ghats receive heavy orographic rainfall
- The leeward side receives less rainfall due to rain shadow effect

3.2 Bay of Bengal Branch

- Enters India through Assam and North-East India
- Causes heavy rainfall in Meghalaya
- Mawsynram and Cherrapunji receive very high rainfall
- Then moves westward along the Himalayas
- Gives rainfall to Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Punjab

4. RAINFALL DISTRIBUTION DURING SOUTHWEST MONSOON

| Region | Rainfall |
|---|---|
| Western Ghats | Very high |
| North-East India | Very high |
| Gangetic Plain | Moderate to high |
| Rajasthan | Low |
| Deccan Plateau | Low to moderate |

5. BREAK IN MONSOON

Sometimes monsoon rainfall stops for a few days.
This is called break in monsoon.

Break occurs when:
- Monsoon trough shifts to Himalayan foothills
- Rainfall increases in Himalayan region
- Plains receive less rainfall

6. WITHDRAWAL OF SOUTHWEST MONSOON

- Withdrawal begins from north-west India in early September
- It withdraws from Rajasthan and Punjab first
- By mid-October, it withdraws from northern half of peninsula
- By early December, it withdraws from the rest of the country

IMD considers withdrawal from extreme north-west India only after 1 September.
Withdrawal from the southern peninsula and the entire country occurs only after 1 October. [54][64]

7. IMPORTANT FACTS

- Southwest monsoon is also called advancing monsoon
- It lasts from June to September
- It gives about 75% to 90% of annual rainfall
- Kerala receives monsoon first
- Arabian Sea branch strikes Western Ghats
- Bay of Bengal branch affects North-East India
- Mawsynram receives highest rainfall
- Withdrawal begins from north-west India
- Break in monsoon means temporary stop of rainfall

PART B - UNIT 4: RETREATING MONSOON AND RAINFALL DISTRIBUTION

1. INTRODUCTION

Retreating monsoon occurs during October and November.
It is also called northeast monsoon.

It is the main rainy season for Tamil Nadu.

2. MECHANISM OF RETREATING MONSOON

- After September, southwest monsoon weakens
- Low pressure trough moves south
- Winds start retreating from north-west India
- By October, winds blow from land to sea
- These winds pick up moisture from Bay of Bengal
- They give rainfall to Tamil Nadu and coastal Andhra Pradesh

3. TAMIL NADU AND RETREATING MONSOON

- Tamil Nadu receives most of its rainfall during retreating monsoon
- This season lasts from October to mid-December
- It accounts for about 48% of Tamil Nadu's annual rainfall
- Coastal districts receive about 60% of their annual rainfall during this season
- Interior districts receive about 40% to 50% of annual rainfall [55][57]

4. CYCLONES DURING RETREATING MONSOON

- Cyclones are common in Bay of Bengal during October and November
- They affect Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha and West Bengal
- Eastern coast is more affected than western coast

5. RAINFALL DISTRIBUTION IN INDIA

| Region | Annual Rainfall |
|---|---|
| Western Ghats | 2000 to 6000 mm |
| North-East India | 2500 to 4000 mm |
| Gangetic Plain | 1000 to 2000 mm |
| Rajasthan Desert | Below 200 mm |
| Deccan Plateau | 500 to 750 mm |

6. AREAS OF HIGH RAINFALL

- Western Ghats
- North-East India
- West Bengal
- Coastal Odisha
- Parts of Himachal Pradesh

7. AREAS OF LOW RAINFALL

- Western Rajasthan
- Kutch region
- Parts of Deccan Plateau
- Ladakh region

8. IMPORTANT FACTS

- Retreating monsoon is also called northeast monsoon
- It occurs from October to November
- Tamil Nadu receives most rainfall during this season
- Cyclones are common in Bay of Bengal
- Mawsynram receives highest rainfall in India
- Western Rajasthan receives lowest rainfall
- Southwest monsoon gives major share of India's rainfall
- Retreating monsoon gives major share of Tamil Nadu's rainfall

PART A - UNIT 5: CLIMATIC REGIONS OF INDIA

1. INTRODUCTION

India has diverse climatic regions due to variations in:
- Latitude
- Altitude
- Distance from the sea
- Relief
- Monsoon winds

Based on Koppen's classification, India has eight major climatic regions.

2. KOPPEN CLASSIFICATION

Koppen classified world climates based on temperature and rainfall.

Major climatic groups:
- A - Tropical climates
- B - Dry climates
- C - Warm temperate climates
- D - Cold climates
- E - Polar climates

3. CLIMATIC REGIONS OF INDIA

| Region | Koppen Code | Area | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monsoon with short dry season | Amw | West Coast, parts of North-East | High rainfall, short dry winter |
| Monsoon with dry summer | As | Coromandel Coast, Tamil Nadu | Rainfall mainly from retreating monsoon |
| Tropical Savanna | Aw | Most of Peninsular India | Dry winter, wet summer |
| Semi-arid Steppe | BShw | Interior Peninsular India, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana | Hot dry summer, low rainfall |
| Hot Desert | BWhw | Western Rajasthan, Thar Desert | Very low rainfall, high temperature |
| Monsoon with dry winter | Cwg | Ganga Plain, North India | Seasonal temperature variation, dry winter |
| Cold humid winter with short summer | Dfc | Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh, parts of North-East | Cold humid winter, short summer |
| Tundra | Et | High Himalayas, Ladakh | Very cold, mosses and lichens |

4. IMPORTANT CLIMATIC REGIONS

4.1 Amw - Monsoon with Short Dry Season

- Found in west coast and parts of North-East India
- Rainfall above 200 cm
- Short dry season in winter
- Kerala, coastal Karnataka, Konkan are examples

4.2 As - Monsoon with Dry Summer

- Found in Coromandel Coast
- Tamil Nadu is the main region
- Rainfall mainly from retreating monsoon
- Dry summer, wet winter

4.3 Aw - Tropical Savanna

- Found in most of Peninsular India
- South of Tropic of Cancer
- Dry winter and wet summer
- Moderate rainfall

4.4 BShw - Semi-arid Steppe

- Found in interior Peninsular India
- Parts of Rajasthan, Gujarat, Punjab and Haryana
- Hot dry summers
- Low and variable rainfall

4.5 BWhw - Hot Desert

- Found in western Rajasthan
- Thar Desert region
- Very low rainfall
- High temperature

4.6 Cwg - Monsoon with Dry Winter

- Found in Ganga Plain
- Parts of North India
- Significant seasonal temperature variation
- Dry winter

4.7 Dfc - Cold Humid Winter with Short Summer

- Found in Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh and parts of North-East
- Cold humid winters
- Short cool summers

4.8 Et - Tundra

- Found in high Himalayas and Ladakh
- Very cold climate
- Mosses and lichens
- Frozen ground

5. IMPORTANT FACTS

- India has eight major climatic regions
- Koppen classification is based on temperature and rainfall
- Tamil Nadu has As type climate
- Coromandel Coast receives rainfall from retreating monsoon
- Thar Desert has BWhw climate
- Ladakh has Et climate
- Ganga Plain has Cwg climate
- Western Ghats have Amw climate

PART A - UNIT: CLIMATE CHANGE AND ITS IMPACTS

1. INTRODUCTION

Climate change refers to long-term changes in temperature, rainfall, wind patterns and
other aspects of the earth's climate.

Main cause: Increase in greenhouse gases due to human activities.

Major greenhouse gases:
1. Carbon dioxide (CO2)
2. Methane (CH4)
3. Nitrous oxide (N2O)
4. Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)

2. CAUSES OF CLIMATE CHANGE

2.1 Burning of Fossil Fuels

- Coal, oil and natural gas
- Release carbon dioxide
- Major source of greenhouse gases

2.2 Deforestation

- Reduces carbon absorption
- Increases CO2 in atmosphere

2.3 Industrialisation

- Releases greenhouse gases
- Increases pollution

2.4 Agriculture

- Methane from paddy fields
- Nitrous oxide from fertilisers

2.5 Urbanisation

- Increases energy consumption
- Increases heat island effect

3. IMPACTS OF CLIMATE CHANGE IN INDIA

3.1 Impact on Monsoon

- Erratic monsoon rainfall
- Delayed onset
- Early withdrawal
- Increased frequency of droughts and floods
- Affects kharif crops

3.2 Impact on Agriculture

- Reduced crop yield
- Increased pest attacks
- Heat stress on crops
- Wheat yield may decline with 1°C rise in temperature
- Affects food security

3.3 Impact on Water Resources

- Melting of Himalayan glaciers
- Reduced snow cover
- Affects Ganga and Brahmaputra river flow
- Groundwater depletion
- Increased water scarcity

3.4 Impact on Coastal Areas

- Sea level rise
- Coastal erosion
- Salinisation of groundwater
- Threat to mangroves
- Displacement of coastal population

3.5 Impact on Forests and Biodiversity

- Shift in forest types
- Loss of biodiversity
- Increased forest fires
- Threat to endemic species

3.6 Impact on Health

- Heat waves
- Vector-borne diseases
- Malnutrition due to crop failure

4. IMPORTANT FACTS

- Climate change is caused mainly by greenhouse gases
- CO2 is the major greenhouse gas
- Monsoon variability affects agriculture
- Himalayan glacier melting affects river flow
- Sea level rise affects coastal areas
- Wheat yield may decline with 1°C rise
- India has 18 biosphere reserves
- Project Tiger launched in 1973
- Project Elephant launched in 1992
