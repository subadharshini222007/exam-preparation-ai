
VOCABULARY

1. One Word Substitution
One word substitution questions require replacing a phrase or a descriptive sentence with a single, precise word. This tests both vocabulary range and the ability to identify the exact term for a given idea, which is a recurring question type in TNPSC's English section.
Bibliophile — a person who loves or collects books. Example: Being a bibliophile, he spends most of his salary on books.
Ambidextrous — able to use both hands equally well. Example: The cricketer is ambidextrous and can bat with either hand.
Autobiography — an account of a person's life written by that person. Example: The former President released his autobiography last year.
Philanthropist — a person who seeks to promote the welfare of others through donations. Example: The philanthropist funded the construction of a new school.
Nomad — a member of a people with no fixed residence who moves from place to place. Example: Nomad tribes in the region move with their herds according to the season.
Chronology — the arrangement of events in the order in which they occurred. Example: The historian presented the events in strict chronology.
Egoist — a person who is excessively focused on themselves. Example: An egoist rarely considers the opinions of others.
Cul-de-sac — a street or passage closed at one end. Example: Their house is located at the end of a quiet cul-de-sac.
 
2. Idioms and Phrases
An idiom is a group of words whose meaning cannot be understood from the individual words alone; it carries a figurative meaning that native usage has fixed over time. TNPSC exams frequently test idioms because they reveal a candidate's familiarity with natural English usage beyond literal translation.
Once in a blue moon — very rarely. Example: He visits his hometown once in a blue moon due to his busy schedule.
Bite the bullet — to face a difficult situation with courage. Example: The company had to bite the bullet and cut costs to survive the crisis.
Break the ice — to initiate conversation in a stiff or awkward setting. Example: The host told a joke to break the ice at the meeting.
Costs an arm and a leg — to be very expensive. Example: That imported car costs an arm and a leg.
Hit the nail on the head — to describe exactly what is causing a situation. Example: Her analysis of the problem hit the nail on the head.
Under the weather — feeling slightly unwell. Example: She stayed home today as she was feeling under the weather.
A blessing in disguise — something that seems bad at first but turns out well. Example: Losing that job was a blessing in disguise, as it led to a better career.
Back to square one — to return to the starting point after a failed attempt. Example: When the plan failed, the team was back to square one.

3. Phrasal Verbs
A phrasal verb combines a verb with a preposition or adverb (or both) to create a meaning different from the original verb. These are common in spoken and written English, and TNPSC often tests them under sentence completion or vocabulary sections.
Bring up — to raise a topic, or to raise a child. Example: She was brought up by her grandparents in a small village.
Carry out — to perform or complete a task. Example: The officials were asked to carry out the survey within a week.
Come across — to find unexpectedly. Example: While cleaning the attic, he came across old family photographs.
Look into — to investigate. Example: The committee promised to look into the complaints raised by residents.
Put off — to postpone. Example: The meeting was put off due to the sudden heavy rain.
Turn down — to reject an offer or request. Example: He turned down the job offer as the salary was low.
Set up — to establish or arrange something. Example: The government plans to set up a new industrial park in the district.
Wipe out — to destroy or eliminate completely. Example: The flood wiped out the entire crop in the low-lying areas.

4. Homophones and Homonyms
Homophones are words that sound alike but have different meanings and often different spellings, while homonyms are words that share the same spelling or pronunciation but differ in meaning. Confusing these is a common error, and TNPSC exams test the ability to choose the correct word for a given context.
Affect / Effect — Affect (verb) means to influence; Effect (noun) means a result. Example: The new policy will affect farmers, and its effect will be seen within a year.
Stationary / Stationery — Stationary means not moving; Stationery refers to writing materials. Example: The vehicle remained stationary while she bought stationery from the shop.
Principal / Principle — Principal means head of an institution or most important; Principle means a fundamental rule. Example: The principal explained the guiding principle behind the new curriculum.
Complement / Compliment — Complement means something that completes; Compliment means a polite expression of praise. Example: Her scarf was a perfect complement to her dress, and she received many compliments.
Council / Counsel — Council is a group that governs or advises; Counsel means advice or a legal advisor. Example: The village council sought the counsel of a legal expert.
 
5. Common Errors in Usage
Spotting errors is a key question type in TNPSC's English section, where a sentence is divided into parts and the candidate must identify the part containing a grammatical mistake. Familiarity with commonly confused word pairs and typical mistakes helps in answering these quickly and accurately.
Less vs Fewer — Use 'less' for uncountable quantities and 'fewer' for countable items. Example: Correct: There are fewer students in the class this year, not 'less students'.
Between vs Among — Use 'between' for two entities and 'among' for more than two. Example: Correct: The prize was divided among the five winners, not 'between'.
Its vs It's — 'Its' shows possession; 'it's' is a contraction of 'it is'. Example: Correct: The dog wagged its tail; it's a friendly animal.
Lay vs Lie — 'Lay' takes an object (to lay something down); 'lie' does not (to lie down oneself). Example: Correct: She lay the book on the table, then went to lie down.
Who vs Whom — 'Who' is a subject pronoun; 'whom' is an object pronoun. Example: Correct: Whom did you meet at the office, and who informed you about it?
6. Proverbs
A proverb is a short, well-known saying that expresses a general truth or piece of advice based on common sense or experience. Understanding proverbs helps in comprehension passages and in questions that ask for the closest meaning of a given saying.
A stitch in time saves nine — timely action prevents bigger problems later. Example: He repaired the small leak immediately, remembering that a stitch in time saves nine.
Actions speak louder than words — what people do matters more than what they say. Example: The new officer proved that actions speak louder than words by clearing the pending files quickly.
Where there is a will, there is a way — determination can overcome any obstacle. Example: Despite the financial hardship, she completed her degree, proving that where there is a will, there is a way.
Do not count your chickens before they hatch — do not assume success before it actually happens. Example: He started celebrating his promotion too early, forgetting not to count his chickens before they hatch.
Every cloud has a silver lining — there is something good in every difficult situation. Example: Losing the contract led them to a better client, proving every cloud has a silver lining.

ERROR SPOTTING

2. Neither of the students (A) / were present (B) / for the exam. (C) / No error (D)
Answer: B – "were" should be "was" (neither... is singular)

DIRECT/INDIRECT SPEECH

## PART 1: DIRECT TO INDIRECT (50 examples)

1. He said, "I am happy."
Answer: He said that he was happy.

2. She said, "I am writing a letter."
Answer: She said that she was writing a letter.

3. He said, "I have finished my work."
Answer: He said that he had finished his work.

4. She said, "I went to the market yesterday."
